## Contract Paraphrase

**D1 (methodology_rigor; mandatory; scored by the methodology seat, not by me).** I read this as asking whether the empirical work would hold up under a careful applied-ML referee: how data are partitioned and handled, how results and their variability are reported, and whether someone could rerun the study from what is supplied. The contract's stage notes make that bar concrete for health-sensing ML: confirmatory results kept apart from exploratory or post-hoc ones; no leakage between the material used to fit, tune or quantize a model and the material used to judge it; uncertainty that respects clustered, dependent data, since many windows from one person are not independent observations; attention to multiple comparisons; runnable reproducibility artifacts; numbers that agree across abstract, text, tables, figures and result files; and effect sizes weighed against seed, resampling-draw and cluster-level spread rather than read off interval signs. From my lens this matters because health-informatics readers usually judge generalization per person rather than per sample, so the unit of analysis has to be visible to them, but the rigor judgment itself stays with the methodology seat. As a mandatory dimension, a block here alone forces at least a major revision and a fatal block forces rejection.

**D2 (domain_accuracy; mandatory; scored by the domain seat).** This asks whether what the paper says about its field is true and current: prior work described faithfully, terminology used correctly, and other studies' results reported without distortion. A paper straddling ML systems and health informatics has two bodies of evidence to be accurate about, and the typical error sits at the seam: a word used in its ML sense where health readers will hear its clinical sense, or a finding from one field restated in the other's vocabulary with its meaning shifted. When I meet a terminology problem I will ask whether it is a factual misstatement (D2, for the domain seat) or a failure to make the intended meaning reachable for an adjacent reader (D4, mine), and I will not score the same defect twice. A block here alone forces at least a major revision; a fatal block forces rejection.

**D3 (argumentative_coherence; mandatory; owned by the devil's-advocate seat, with the methodology seat also eligible).** This asks whether the central thesis holds together: conclusions follow from the evidence actually offered, sections do not contradict one another, and no reasoning error, such as over-generalizing from one setting, reading a non-significant difference as equivalence, or letting a proxy metric stand in for the outcome it approximates, undercuts the main argument. Applied ML-for-health work usually has to step from what was measured, typically offline performance on a benchmark, to what that means for people or practice; whether the step is logically licensed is D3's question, while whether it is explained so that an adjacent reader cannot over-read it is mine. A block here alone forces at least a major revision and a fatal block forces rejection; separately, a majority view that two or more mandatory dimensions sit at warn or worse also leads to a major revision.

**D4 (cross_disciplinary_relevance; high priority; the dimension this seat alone scores).** I take this as two linked questions. Accessibility: can a reader from a neighbouring field, whether a health-informatics or clinical-adjacent researcher facing quantization and on-device details or a systems engineer facing the health framing, follow the motivation, understand each key term in the sense the authors intend, and leave knowing what the results do and do not show? Substantiation: when the paper reaches across the boundary, saying a systems result matters for health use or that a health premise justifies a technical choice, is that claim carried by evidence in the paper or by the other field's own literature and standards? Because the contract places clinical validity outside this review and treats the work as a research prototype, I also read D4 as covering how practical implications and safety scope are framed: the absence of clinical evaluation is acceptable, but framing that lets a reader mistake benchmark results for clinical or deployment evidence is not. Being high priority, a block here forces at least a major revision and a warn at least a minor one; this dimension has no fatal tier.

**D5 (writing_and_structure; normal priority; scored by the EIC).** This covers how the manuscript is organized, how clearly it explains itself, the quality of its figures and tables, and whether it follows the venue's conventions. In a two-field paper clarity depends on the audience (definitions and notation placed before they are needed, axes and units labelled, category definitions restated where results are read), so it overlaps with D4. I will draw the line this way: a presentation problem that only slows reading belongs to D5; one that would lead an adjacent-field reader to a wrong conclusion I treat under D4. On its own, even a block here reaches only the minor-revision condition.

**D6 (venue_fit_and_contribution; mandatory; scored by the EIC).** This asks whether the work belongs in its target venue and gives that venue's readers something original and significant. For a paper spanning two communities, significance has to be argued to both: what is new relative to ML-systems work on compressed on-device models, and what is new or useful relative to health-informatics work on sensor time series. A contribution billed as interdisciplinary should look novel from each side, not novel on one side with an application label borrowed from the other. No venue criteria are bound in this run, so I make no venue-alignment judgment and leave this score to the EIC. A block here alone forces at least a major revision; a fatal block forces rejection.

## Scoring Plan

### D4: cross_disciplinary_relevance

dimension_id: D4
what_to_look_for: Read the manuscript twice, once as a health-informatics or clinical-adjacent reader meeting the ML-systems content and once as an ML or embedded-systems reader meeting the health framing, and check that (1) the problem is framed so both can say what is predicted, from which signal, over what time window, and why the on-device or quantized constraint matters for the health-sensing use; (2) terms whose meaning shifts between fields are defined at first use in the intended sense (e.g., calibration as quantization-range data vs probability calibration vs sensor calibration; validation as a held-out split vs clinical validation; sensitivity, accuracy, real-time), systems concepts a health reader cannot be assumed to know (what quantization changes and what can degrade) are explained to the depth the results need, and any label categories or thresholds attributed to domain practice are cited to that practice; (3) ML metrics (per-class and macro scores, confusion structure, calibration error) and systems metrics (latency, size, energy) are translated into operational meaning, e.g., which confusions a domain reader would weigh most where classes are ordered, and whether latency differences matter relative to the signal's sampling interval; (4) boundary-crossing claims (a systems result said to matter for health use, a domain premise said to justify a design choice, privacy, offline-availability or equity benefits attributed to on-device inference) are carried by the paper's own evidence or by citations from the other field, not asserted; (5) practical implications and safety scope are explicit wherever results could be read as guidance (abstract, conclusion, figure captions, example outputs) and consistent with the research-prototype, no-clinical-validity scope this review operates under, where missing clinical evaluation is not a deficiency but inviting over-reading is; (6) limitations give an adjacent reader what is needed to avoid over-generalizing (population and dataset provenance and representativeness, benchmark vs real-world sensing conditions, no outcome evaluation); isolated cosmetic lapses such as one standard acronym left unexpanded stay at pass.
what_triggers_block: A systemic or headline-level gap that would lead an adjacent-field reader to a materially wrong conclusion and cannot be fixed by rewording alone because it needs re-scoped claims, new evidence, or grounding in the other field's literature; that is, any of: health-facing implications (user or patient benefit, real-world or clinical readiness, safety, alerting suitability, care or treatment decisions) presented as supported findings when the evidence is offline or benchmark performance; abstract or conclusion framing that contradicts the research-prototype, no-clinical-validity scope, which a disclaimer elsewhere does not cure; a term the central result depends on left undefined or used in conflicting senses across sections so the main finding is misread across fields; or an interdisciplinary contribution claim, or a domain premise the core design rests on, supported by neither evidence nor any citation from the other field.
what_triggers_warn: Localized gaps that leave the central result and its scope correctly understood but make the paper harder to use, or easier to over-read, for one of the two audiences, each fixable by wording, definitions or added citations without changing any claim or analysis, e.g.: several cross-field terms or acronyms undefined at first use; metrics reported with no sentence on what they mean for a domain reader; one boundary-crossing sentence worded more strongly than the paper's own hedging elsewhere, or supported only by same-field citations; a correct scope or safety statement missing from one of abstract, conclusion or relevant captions; or limitations omitting one item an adjacent reader needs (population representativeness, benchmark vs real-world conditions, retrospective design).
