\begin{abstract}
How does calibration-data coverage affect minority-class performance and
deployment cost in a compact, quantized glucose trend classifier, and which
changes improve it once that is fixed? We study both on \project, an
open-source Android and iOS prototype that maps 12 five-minute continuous
glucose monitor (CGM) readings to one of five rate-of-change classes 15
minutes ahead. On a corrected, provenance-masked protocol, a
2,909-parameter CNN trained at five seeds on public Weinstock data
prepared by GlucoBench was quantized with four calibration-selection
strategies at two budgets and three draws (100 INT8 artifacts). The toolchain-default sequential
selection covers two participants, caps the INT8 input range at
251~\mgdl, and costs 13.4 points of held-out directional recall (95\% CI
10.3--16.9) against a uniform random selection of the same size, while raw
accuracy rises. Any randomized selection restores float-level recall;
range stratification, a five-fold budget, and the observer choice add
essentially nothing; clip-only controls attribute the loss to input-range
clipping; and the fix costs nothing at deployment. The effect reproduces
on two further cohorts, and difference-encoded inputs make quantization
nearly calibration-proof. Five staged accuracy sweeps followed, each
judged by one pre-registered read. A macro-F1 winner passed every gate
but gained only on the majority class and was rejected; a model trained
only on the public DiaData collection passed a directional endpoint; a
TCN broke the stable-recall floor; a fourth round reproduced the shipped
model exactly; and a position-preserving flatten head passed all gates
zero-shot on a never-read cohort (RT-CGM, 12.7M windows; $+0.20$ points,
CI $+0.11$ to $+0.29$) and now ships in both applications. \project is a
research demonstration, not a medical device.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A CGM sampled every five minutes produces 12 observations per hour. These
readings can be used to predict a near-future glucose value, event, or trend
\cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}. Here, we study such predictions for research
purposes; they are not intended to guide treatment.

These targets define different learning problems --- value regression, event
prediction, and trend classification --- and published studies further differ
in horizon, contextual inputs, cohort, split construction, and metric. Reviews,
comparison indices, public benchmarks, and cross-dataset experiments document
these differences and help separate within-dataset performance from transfer
\cite{liu2023systematic,facchinetti2011index,
sergazinov2024glucobench,ghimire2024generalize}; scores from incompatible
protocols should not be compared directly.

Commercial CGM trend arrows are related but not equivalent targets: they
summarize recent or current direction and rate using device-specific
definitions \cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend}, whereas
\project derives each label from the change between the current sample and the
sample 15 minutes later --- a compact research representation that neither
reproduces a manufacturer's algorithm nor provides a dosing rule.

Deployment raises further questions. Conversion may change a model's outputs,
the client may need different input handling, and lower precision does not
guarantee lower latency on a given device. Class imbalance can hide conversion
errors: accuracy can increase as predictions concentrate on a majority class
even while minority-class recall declines \cite{brodersen2010balanced}.

The research question is: \emph{how does calibration-data coverage affect
minority-class performance and deployment cost in compact glucose trend
classifiers?} Here, coverage refers to the input values and trajectories
represented during quantizer calibration, not calibration of predicted
probabilities. An earlier revision of this work could only motivate the
question: a provenance audit traced the shipped INT8 artifact's input range
to a single participant's 17.5 hours of data, whose maximum set a ceiling
below readings found in a quarter of training windows, but no comparison of
calibration selections existed. This revision runs that comparison under a
prespecified protocol, frozen --- hypotheses, samplers, endpoint,
missing-class rules, and environment --- before any held-out label was read.

The study holds the architecture, loss, quantizer, and data split fixed and
varies only calibration selection: four strategies (the toolchain-default
sequential order, uniform random, participant-balanced random, and
participant-balanced range coverage), two budgets (200 and 1,000 windows,
nested), three random draws, and five training seeds, giving 100 INT8
artifacts evaluated against their five float sources, plus a ten-artifact
min--max-observer arm. The evaluation protocol is corrected relative to the
earlier revision: split-role provenance masks remove context-only targets,
a gap-fill mask removes windows touched by interpolation of missing
readings, and held-out-participant evaluation is separated from temporal
evaluation on training participants. With calibration settled, the work
then asks which model and data changes improve directional recall, through
five staged accuracy sweeps that each spend one pre-registered
confirmatory read. This paper reports:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a corrected, provenance-masked evaluation protocol for
    GlucoBench-formatted CGM windows, with eligible-window and class-support
    audits published alongside the code;
    \item a controlled answer to the coverage question: randomizing a
    200-window calibration selection recovers all of the directional recall
    the default sequential selection loses (13.4 points, 95\% CI
    10.3--16.9), while range-aware stratification, a five-fold budget, and
    an observer change add nothing measurable;
    \item mechanism controls that attribute the sequential-arm loss to
    input-range clipping rather than input-grid resolution or internal
    quantization; and
    \item deployment-cost accounting: every INT8 artifact has identical
    size, sampler and export times are fractions of a second, so the
    corrected calibration is free at deployment time. Prior
    single-device latency observations are retained as historical context;
    and
    \item five accuracy sweeps under that one-read discipline
    (Sections~\ref{sec:sweep1}--\ref{sec:sweep5}): a macro-F1 winner
    rejected because its gain was majority-class only, external DiaData
    training, a measured label-noise ceiling, and a position-preserving
    flatten head confirmed zero-shot on a never-read cohort, which now
    ships in both applications.
\end{itemize}

All source code, tests, synthetic replay data, and shipped model artifacts are
available in the project repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/mohamedmenasy/GlucoEdge}}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Forecasting tasks, horizons, and inputs}

CGM forecasting has expanded from autoregressive value prediction on sensor
history \cite{reifman2007predictive} to recurrent and attention models with
contextual inputs \cite{mirshekarian2019lstm}, recurrent variance estimation
\cite{martinsson2020variance}, and convolutional--recurrent fusion of CGM,
insulin, and meals \cite{li2020crnn}. Architecture and input modality change
together across these studies, so their results do not isolate model family;
cross-dataset evaluation asks instead whether a trained model survives a change
of dataset, cohort, and collection process \cite{ghimire2024generalize}.
Table~\ref{tab:relatedwork} summarizes these design differences, separating
offline predictive evaluation from reported device execution; it omits scores
that would be misleading to compare across protocols.

\subsection{Benchmarks and evaluation protocols}

The split determines what a study can claim about generalization:
subject-dependent splits evaluate people represented during training,
held-out-participant splits evaluate unseen people in the same dataset, and
cross-dataset evaluation also changes the cohort and pipeline. GlucoBench
standardizes preprocessing, splits, and baselines across five public datasets
\cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}; experiments across four datasets directly test
changes in data source \cite{ghimire2024generalize}. \project uses GlucoBench's
largest type-1 diabetes cohort, Weinstock, whose source study collected blinded
CGM from older adults with long-standing type-1 diabetes
\cite{weinstock2016risk}.

Nearby and overlapping windows are dependent, so time-series splitting requires
care: cross-validation validity for autoregressive prediction depends on the
residual structure \cite{bergmeir2018crossvalidation}, leakage can answer a less
demanding question than the one claimed \cite{kapoor2023leakage}, and even with
a fixed split, training and implementation choices contribute benchmark
variance \cite{bouthillier2021variance}. Results therefore need to state the
participant, temporal, and dataset boundaries used in evaluation.

\subsection{Trend representations and class imbalance}

Commercial trend arrows summarize recent or current movement using
device-specific categories whose rate bands and look-back intervals differ
\cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend}. \project uses these rate
bands to define a research target from the sample 15 minutes after the window
--- a future label, not a reproduction of any commercial arrow algorithm, and
no basis for treatment decisions.

Most Weinstock labels are stable and faster changes are rare, so overall
accuracy weights the stable class by its prevalence. Macro-averaged recall
(balanced accuracy in this single-label setting) gives each class equal weight
and makes minority-class degradation visible \cite{brodersen2010balanced},
though it still does not establish event-level or clinical performance.

No published baseline exists for this exact task (five rate-of-change
classes at a future sample, scored on held-out participants). The
nearest classification results predict glucose
\emph{level} bands, with recalls of 0.85--0.96 at 15-minute and 1-hour
horizons under within-patient splits \cite{kistkins2023classification};
level bands inherit the level's autocorrelation, three classes
are coarser than five rate bands, and within-patient splits exploit
person-specific dynamics a held-out protocol denies.
Forecasting work evaluates RMSE, where direction surfaces only as a
safety penalty on incoherent predicted variation
\cite{debois2019coherent}.

\subsection{Edge inference and quantization}

One-dimensional CNNs extract local temporal features with compact feed-forward
networks \cite{kiranyaz2021survey}. Mobile inference systems can reduce
execution cost through compression and runtime-specific scheduling
\cite{lane2016deepx}, but latency is a property of model, runtime, and hardware
together: Li et al. reported Android timing for a glucose forecasting model
\cite{li2020crnn}, and smartphone and tiny-device benchmarks measure specified
workloads and platforms because model size alone does not determine speed
\cite{ignatov2019aibenchmark,banbury2021mlperftiny}.

Integer quantization approximates floating-point tensors with discrete values
parameterized by a scale and zero point \cite{jacob2018quantization}; static
post-training quantization estimates activation ranges from calibration inputs
before conversion \cite{pytorchPT2E2026,googleRepresentativeData}. Low-bit
studies show that clipping, range estimation, and rounding choices can
determine the resulting error \cite{banner2019ptq,nagel2020adaround}, with
specialized methods for small calibration sets
\cite{hubara2021smallcalibration} --- findings that support checking converted
outputs and per-class metrics alongside model size.

\project connects public preprocessing with conversion parity, class-wise
evaluation, and offline replay on one Android and one iOS device; it does not
establish live-sensor, clinical, or cross-hardware performance.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $g_t$ be the CGM value at time $t$, in \mgdl, with observations every five
minutes. The input is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t = [g_{t-55}, g_{t-50}, \ldots, g_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{12}.
\end{equation}
The future rate of change is computed from a point outside the input window:
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \frac{g_{t+15}-g_t}{15}\quad [\mgdl/\mathrm{min}].
\end{equation}
The class $y_t$ is assigned by
\begin{equation}
y_t =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{falling\_fast}, & r_t \leq -2,\\
\texttt{falling},       & -2 < r_t \leq -1,\\
\texttt{stable},        & -1 < r_t < 1,\\
\texttt{rising},        & 1 \leq r_t < 2,\\
\texttt{rising\_fast}, & r_t \geq 2.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The 12 readings span 55 elapsed minutes; ``one-hour history'' refers to 12
samples at a five-minute cadence. The history length and 15-minute horizon
are fixed engineering choices for this prototype. The five-class target is a
simplification based on two samples and does not describe the full intervening
trajectory.
The boundaries use rate bands commonly represented by CGM trend arrows, while
collapsing device-specific arrow taxonomies into five classes
\cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend}. No threshold was optimized on
the test labels.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset and preprocessing}

The experiments use the Weinstock data distributed through GlucoBench. The
source cohort comprised older adults with long-standing type-1 diabetes and
blinded CGM collection \cite{weinstock2016risk}. GlucoBench reports 200
participants before preprocessing and 192 afterwards, with a five-minute
sampling interval \cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}; our audit of the formatter
output reproduces both counts. The configuration used here disables scaling
and retains glucose in raw \mgdl. The formatter rounds timestamps to the
minute, splits each participant's record at gaps longer than 45 minutes, drops
segments with fewer than 240 original readings, and linearly interpolates each
remaining segment onto a five-minute grid that starts at its first reading.
Each segment is tagged with an \texttt{id\_segment}. Of the 579,782 grid
values, 75.8\% coincide with an original reading. A further 19.8\% fall
between two consecutive readings no more than 7.5 minutes apart, because
reading times drift relative to the grid; 4.4\% fill one or more missing
readings. Because interpolation precedes windowing, interpolated values may
occur among the inputs or at samples used to form targets. The configured
split holds out 10\% of participants (20 people) for
an out-of-distribution component and forms temporal validation and test
components from the remaining participants. The formatter combines the test
components into one frame; Section~\ref{sec:eligible} separates them again
by row ownership, and all results report the held-out-participant and
temporal components separately.

Windows are generated independently within each $(\texttt{id},
\texttt{id\_segment})$ group and ordered by time. A window is emitted only when
all 12 input points and the three following horizon steps fit in the same
segment. The stride is one reading, matching an application that updates every
five minutes. This rule prevents a sensor gap or participant boundary from
being treated as a continuous trajectory. Adjacent stride-one windows overlap
heavily and are statistically dependent. The resulting frames yielded 424,462 training,
55,289 validation, and 127,165 aggregate test windows from 172, 127, and 186
participants, respectively. The test windows comprise 57,068 from the 20
held-out participants and 70,097 from temporal segments of 166 training
participants.

Table~\ref{tab:distribution} shows the resulting class imbalance. Both fast
classes exceed 13,000 training windows. A preliminary 20-epoch run obtained
test recalls of 0.5333 and 0.5380 for \texttt{falling\_fast} and
\texttt{rising\_fast}, respectively. Neither met the predeclared condition for
collapsing the classes: fewer than 200 training examples or recall below 0.30.
The deployed model therefore retains all five classes. Test recall informed
this five-versus-three decision, so it must be treated as an exploratory
development choice rather than independent validation.

\subsection{Split roles, observation mask, and eligible windows}
\label{sec:eligible}

GlucoBench repeats rows from a preceding split at the top of validation and
in-distribution test frames so sequence models can form initial histories.
The earlier revision of this work let every prefix row start a window, so
13.6\% of aggregate test windows scored targets in validation-owned rows and
35.1\% of validation windows duplicated training windows
(Table~\ref{tab:provenance}). The corrected protocol assigns every formatter
row to the earliest split that lists it and scores a window in a split only
when its anchor (last input sample) and all three forecast rows are owned by
that split. Earlier-split readings may serve as causal history among the
inputs. Scored $(\text{participant}, \text{segment}, \text{target time})$
keys are asserted disjoint across splits.

Because the formatter interpolates before windowing, a second mask removes
gap filling: a grid value is retained only if it lies on or between two
original readings at most 7.5 minutes apart. An audit showed that requiring
exact coincidence with an original reading would discard roughly 40\% of
windows for grid drift unrelated to missing data, so the gap-fill rule is
the primary mask and exact coincidence is a sensitivity tier. A window is
eligible when its 12 inputs and future endpoint are all gap-free; the two
intermediate forecast steps are not label inputs and are not masked.
Table~\ref{tab:eligible} shows the result. Eligible training windows retain
all five classes with at least 10,330 examples
(\texttt{falling\_fast}); the calibration pool keeps 127 of 172 development
participants.

\subsection{Model and training}

The \texttt{TrendCNN} accepts tensors of shape $(B,1,12)$. It applies a
one-dimensional convolution from 1 to 24 channels, a second convolution from
24 to 36 channels, ReLU after each layer, adaptive average pooling over time,
and a 36-to-5 linear head. Both convolutions use kernel size 3, stride 1, and
padding 1. The network contains 2,909 trainable parameters.

Training uses inverse-frequency class-weighted cross-entropy, batch size 64,
and Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 20 epochs
\cite{kingma2015adam,paszke2019pytorch}. The study trains five checkpoints
with seeds 0--4 on the eligible windows of Section~\ref{sec:eligible}; the
fixed split does not change with the training seed. Validation loss was
observed after every epoch; there was no early stopping or test-time
selection. Classification metrics were computed with scikit-learn and the
study's own confusion-count code \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. Because the
test distribution is dominated by \texttt{stable}, the primary summary is
the unweighted mean recall of the four directional classes (minority macro
recall); five-class macro recall, equivalent to balanced accuracy, is also
reported \cite{brodersen2010balanced}.

Two input-dependent baselines use the same eligible windows. The recent-slope
baseline maps $(g_t - g_{t-15})/15$, computed from the inputs alone, through
the same five thresholds. The logistic-regression baseline is a multinomial
model on the 12 readings with training-only standardization, balanced class
weights, and fixed $C=1$; it converged. The constant-\texttt{stable}
classifier shows the effect of prevalence. The calibration study starts
from the originally shipped artifact (seed 0, historical protocol); the
applications' bundled models were later replaced by study and sweep
winners (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Conversion and Deployment}

\subsection{Shipped artifacts (historical protocol)}

The originally shipped application checkpoint was converted with LiteRT Torch: float
conversion with a fixed $(1,1,12)$ input signature, INT8 conversion through
PyTorch 2 Export (PT2E) with LiteRT Torch's
\texttt{get\_symmetric\_quantization\_config} helper
(\texttt{is\_per\_channel=True}, \texttt{is\_dynamic=False}). Despite the
helper's name, only the weights are quantized symmetrically, per channel;
activations, including the model input, use per-tensor affine quantization
whose ranges a histogram observer selects to minimize quantization error
\cite{pytorchPT2E2026}. The first 200 validation windows serve as calibration
inputs, taken in the dataset's participant/segment and time order with no
randomization or range balancing; Section~\ref{sec:provenance} shows that all
200 come from one participant. The converted input scale is 0.9408~\mgdl\ per
code with zero point $-128$, a representable range of 0--239.9~\mgdl\ ---
starting at zero because the observer always includes zero, which leaves the
codes below the lowest CGM reading unused.

\subsection{Calibration experiment design}
\label{sec:design}

The study freezes the checkpoint, quantizer, and conversion pipeline and
varies only which validation windows calibrate the histogram observer. The
calibration pool is the 27,109 eligible validation windows; no sampler
reads future labels, and selection code is tested to be invariant under
label shuffling. Four strategies are compared. \emph{Sequential} takes the
first windows in deterministic (participant, segment, time) order --- the
toolchain-default pattern that produced the shipped artifact.
\emph{Uniform random} is a seeded permutation of the pool.
\emph{Participant-balanced random} fixes a shuffled participant order per
draw and cycles it round-robin, taking one window from a fixed
per-participant permutation each visit and skipping exhausted participants.
\emph{Participant-balanced range coverage} reuses exactly the same
participant allocation but, within each participant, cycles through
low/middle/high range cells defined by the pool's own 10th and 90th
percentiles of per-window input minimum and maximum, so it differs from the
balanced-random arm only in within-participant input-range coverage.

Each strategy is drawn at budgets of 200 and 1,000 windows, with the
200-window set nested as the first 200 of the 1,000-window ordering.
Stochastic strategies use three seeded draws (101--103); all selections are
shared across the five model seeds. This yields 100 INT8 artifacts (5 seeds
$\times$ [2 sequential + 3 strategies $\times$ 2 budgets $\times$ 3 draws]).
A mechanism-control arm re-quantizes two cells (sequential-200 and
range-coverage-200, draw 101) with a min--max activation observer in place
of the histogram observer, adding 10 artifacts. For every calibration set
the study records participants, segments, unique input readings, input
extremes and quantiles, and occupied range cells, because a nominal window
count can cover few distinct readings; for every artifact it records input
quantization parameters, size, and SHA-256.

\subsection{Client implementations}

For input scale $s$ and zero point $z$, both clients use the affine form
\begin{equation}
    q = \operatorname{clip}\left(\operatorname{round}(x/s)+z,
    -128,127\right), \qquad \hat{x}=s(q-z).
\end{equation}
Quantization parameters are read from the model metadata rather than
hardcoded. All three clients divide in single precision and round exact
half-way values to the even integer --- Python with \texttt{numpy.round},
Kotlin with \texttt{kotlin.math.round}, Swift with
\texttt{rounded(.toNearestOrEven)} \cite{numpyRound,kotlinRound,swiftRounding}
--- so they produce identical integer inputs for any glucose value. (The July
device tests ran an earlier Kotlin version that rounded ties toward positive
infinity; the golden-vector inputs, given to 0.1~\mgdl, contain no tie at the
shipped input scale, so the expected outputs are unchanged.)

The Kotlin/Jetpack Compose application replays a bundled synthetic trace
through a 12-reading ring buffer that resets after a cadence gap, mirroring
training-time segment boundaries. Every complete window is classified
locally; the merged Android manifest is tested to ensure it does not request
the \texttt{INTERNET} permission. The float artifact is the default and INT8
can be selected for comparison. Real devices use LiteRT 2.1.0
\texttt{CompiledModel} on CPU \cite{googleLiteRTCompiledModel}; on the tested
Apple-Silicon-hosted Android emulator, native model creation hit an
illegal-instruction fault in a CPU feature probe for the Arm Scalable Matrix
Extension, uncatchable from Kotlin, so emulators are selected before runtime
initialization and use the classic interpreter with XNNPACK disabled --- a
workaround for the tested environment, not a statement about LiteRT
configurations generally.

A SwiftUI iOS application mirrors the Android client: it bundles the same two
artifact files and synthetic trace by reference to the Android asset
directory (byte-identical models on both platforms) and runs them through
LiteRT's Swift \texttt{CompiledModel} API on CPU with the LiteRT 2.3.0
release binaries. Replay, the gap-resetting window buffer, the model switch,
and the latency statistics follow the Kotlin implementation. iOS has no
equivalent of Android's \texttt{INTERNET} permission; a source scan fails CI
if networking APIs appear in the application code --- a weaker guard than the
Android manifest check.

\section{Experimental Protocol}

Evaluation uses two disjoint components from Section~\ref{sec:eligible}:
the 46,376 eligible windows of the 20 held-out participants (primary) and
the 37,361 eligible temporal windows of 166 training participants
(secondary). They are never concatenated. The protocol --- hypotheses,
samplers, budgets, endpoint, bootstrap, and missing-class rules --- was
frozen and committed before any held-out label was read, in
\texttt{paper/results/} \texttt{calibration-coverage/protocol.json}. Because an
earlier revision of this work evaluated on an overlapping aggregate frame
and the five-class decision was informed by a historical test run, the
study is a locked, corrected reanalysis of previously touched data, not a
fresh test set.

A provenance audit (\texttt{experiments/split\_audit.py}) maps every window
to the formatter rows it uses and assigns each row to the earliest split
that lists it. It reads row identities and timestamps for all splits but
glucose values only from training and validation rows. It also records the
composition of the shipped calibration set and the shipped INT8 model's
input quantization parameters.

If $n_c$ is class support, $N=\sum_c n_c$, and $R_c$ is class recall, then
\begin{equation}
    A=\sum_{c=1}^{5}\frac{n_c}{N}R_c,
    \qquad R_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{c=1}^{5}R_c,
\end{equation}
and the primary endpoint is the unweighted mean recall of the four
directional classes. The prespecified primary contrast is range-coverage
versus participant-balanced random at budget 200, which isolates
within-participant input-range coverage from participant representation.
Secondary contrasts are range coverage versus uniform random, 1,000 versus
200 within each strategy, and uniform random versus sequential at each
budget. A recall is undefined on zero support and never replaced by zero.

Uncertainty comes from a paired participant-cluster bootstrap: 2,000 draws
with a fixed analysis seed resample participants with replacement, keep
every selected participant's complete windows, recompute each arm's
confusion counts, and average the paired differences over the five model
seeds and three calibration draws inside each bootstrap draw. Percentile
95\% intervals are conditional on the fitted models and calibration sets.
Overlapping windows are never resampled individually. If more than 5\% of
draws have an undefined endpoint, the interval is omitted.

Two input-grid controls separate mechanisms. The float-QDQ control feeds
each INT8 artifact's quantize--dequantize image of the inputs --- using
that artifact's own scale and zero point --- through the float model, which
measures the input-grid intervention alone. The clip-only control clips
inputs to the artifact's representable range without re-gridding, which
separates range truncation from input resolution. Clipping is counted with
the actual quantization operation (round half-to-even, then saturate), per
input slot and per window, with true-class breakdowns on the evaluation
components only. These comparisons describe the measured error components;
they are not an additive causal decomposition of every error.

File size is the exact serialized artifact size. The device observations
below predate this study and describe the shipped artifacts only; none of
the 110 new artifacts has been device-timed. Device latency is measured
inside the application's \texttt{classify} method with
\texttt{System.nanoTime}: buffer writes, input quantization when needed,
model execution, and output reading/dequantization, stopping before softmax,
class selection, and construction of the returned prediction (model loading
and UI updates excluded). Measurements were recorded during replay on a
Samsung Galaxy S22 Ultra (SM-S908E, Android 16); the float statistics use the
latest 100 inferences, the recorded INT8 statistics 21 after a model switch,
and p95 is the sorted sample at zero-based index $\lfloor0.95(n-1)\rfloor$,
so the estimates differ in rank resolution. These timings measure neither
the full classification call nor the kernel alone, and the runs were not
controlled for a benchmark comparison.

The iOS client times the same region with the monotonic
\texttt{DispatchTime} clock, recorded on an iPhone 18 Pro Max (iPhone19,3,
iOS 27.0.1) during 16$\times$ replay of the synthetic trace (one inference
every 312.5 ms), the latest 100 inferences per model in two rounds, debug
application build over the prebuilt release LiteRT binary. A separate
test-runner benchmark calls each model 100 times back to back after one
warm-up call, in three runs, isolating the timed region from the replay
tick interval. Calibration preparation time, peak memory, model-load time,
and energy were not measured.

Cross-runtime parity was tested with 20 synthetic golden windows, four selected
from each float-predicted class and including values above the INT8 input range.
The test also verifies the SHA-256 hash of each bundled artifact. Float logits
must match the Python output within $10^{-5}$; dequantized INT8 outputs are
required to match bit-for-bit because both sides dequantize identical integer
outputs with the same float32 operation. The iOS suite applies the same
vectors and tolerances and also checks the class-name order. It runs on the
physical iPhone, on the iOS simulator, and in continuous integration.

\subsection{Accuracy sweep protocol}
\label{sec:sweepprotocol}

The second research question asks which model and training-data changes
raise the directional recall of the bundled 8-bit integer (INT8) model.
Five rounds of staged greedy selection addressed it
(Section~\ref{sec:sweepresults}). Each stage trains a few arms that
differ in one factor, compares their seed means, and carries the winner
forward; a final stage picks one seed. Selection reads only the 27,109
eligible Weinstock validation windows of Section~\ref{sec:eligible},
whatever the training data, with three seeds per arm in rounds one and
two and five from round three on. Arms within 0.1 point of each
other are tied and the cheaper arm wins (fewer parameters first). Round
three picked its final seed by strict argmax instead, a disclosed
deviation; round five applied the tie rule at every pick, including the
seed and the kept epoch.

Round one selected on validation macro-F1 and confirmed on held-out
macro-F1. Its outcome (Section~\ref{sec:sweep1}) changed the endpoint:
from round two on, selection and confirmation use directional recall, the
mean recall (sensitivity) of the four non-stable classes, guarded by a
stable-recall floor. At selection an arm is disqualified if its mean
validation stable recall falls more than 5 points (rounds two to four)
or 2 points (round five) below the champion's. The champion is the pair
bundled when a round began: the delta-encoded pair (Table~\ref{tab:delta})
in rounds one and two and the round-two winner in rounds three to five
(Table~\ref{tab:variants}). Its validation references come from its
bundled INT8 artifact on the same windows (0.519 directional and 0.563
stable recall for the delta-encoded pair, 0.533 and 0.548 for the
round-two pair); stage tables report float checkpoints.

From round two on, three gates decide a candidate: (1) its INT8 model
beats the champion's INT8 model on the endpoint, with the 95\% interval's
lower bound above $-0.5$ points; (2) its INT8 stable recall is at most 2
points below the champion's; (3) its INT8 directional recall is within
0.5 points of its own float. Round one applied gate~1 to macro-F1, used
the float-proximity rule as its second gate, and reserved its third gate
for ensembles. A round spends at most one confirmatory read. Its
protocol file (\texttt{protocol\_sweep\_r$N$.json}: selection trail,
candidate, champion SHA-256 hashes, prediction, endpoint, gates,
bootstrap seed and pairing rule) is committed before any protected label
is read, and the evaluation code refuses to run until it is. Rounds one
to three read the Weinstock held-out component (primary) and the
temporal component (secondary), and from round two the PEDAP (Pediatric
Artificial Pancreas trial) cohort as a descriptive probe. Round five
moved its primary endpoint to RT-CGM, a cohort no round had evaluated,
and declared the Weinstock components and PEDAP non-gating secondaries.
A candidate whose converted artifacts are SHA-256-identical to the
champion's spends no read (round four's outcome, made a rule in round
five), so four rounds spent one read each and round four none.
Table~\ref{tab:readledger} lists every read of held-out, temporal and
RT-CGM labels in this work. No protocol stated when a gate-passing
candidate may be overridden. The two cases that arose fit one rule:
override only if the candidate loses directional recall, the quantity
the application reports, on the primary component (round one), not for
losses on components the protocol declared non-gating (round five).

Round one trained on the eligible Weinstock training windows; its
pooling stage added the dubosson and hall training windows. Rounds two to
five drew on DiaData v3 \cite{cinar2026diadata}, using only its raw
(non-imputed) five-minute archive. An overlap and sensor audit
(\path{diadata_audit_r1.json}) admitted six type~1 diabetes (T1D)
cohorts with 1,001 subjects and excluded DiaData's copies of the
Weinstock and dubosson studies. Five admitted cohorts (CITY, DCLP3,
REPLACE-BG, SENCE and WISDM; 898 subjects) fed training; PEDAP (103
subjects) trained no model. Mixtures were capped near 15 million
training windows per arm by seeded whole-subject sampling, so no subject
is split. Round three reopened RT-CGM, DiaData's label for the Juvenile
Diabetes Research Foundation (JDRF) continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)
randomized trial, for one training arm, and round five used it as its
primary endpoint. Table~\ref{tab:cohorts} characterizes every cohort.

All sweep models take the delta-anchor input of Table~\ref{tab:delta}
(11 consecutive differences and the last reading divided by 16) over a
12-reading window, except arms that change the input. Training uses Adam
with learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 20 epochs and keeps the final epoch.
Round one trained with batch size 64 on the CPU, as the calibration study
did. Rounds two to five trained with batch size 512 on an Apple-silicon
graphics processor through Metal Performance Shaders (MPS): a pre-sweep
smoke run accepted the largest of 512, 256 and 128 whose mean validation
directional recall stayed within 1 point of batch 64 (512 scored 0.513
and 64 scored 0.506, two seeds each), and two same-seed MPS runs
reproduced exactly. The loss is the study's inverse-frequency
class-weighted cross-entropy (CE) except where a stage varies it. Round
five's recipe arm decays the learning rate to zero on a cosine schedule
over the 20 epochs \cite{loshchilov2017sgdr} and keeps the epoch with
the highest validation directional recall among epochs that meet the
stable floor.

Each candidate was converted as in Section~\ref{sec:design} with two of
the study's calibration selections, rebuilt on delta-encoded inputs:
sequential-200 (diagnostic) and uniform-200, draw 101 (the candidate
artifact). Both come from the study's pool, the 27,109 eligible Weinstock
validation windows, with the study's index lists, so the validation split
served both for selection and for INT8 calibration. The round-two winner
and every later candidate trained on no Weinstock window, so their INT8
calibration is cross-cohort. (The round-five protocol file describes the
pool as Weinstock training windows; the code and the pool hash show the
validation pool.) Confirmatory contrasts pair both models' predictions on
the same windows, keyed by participant and anchor time and identical by
construction from round three on, and use the paired participant-cluster
bootstrap described above (2,000 draws, a seed frozen per round). Each
contrast compares one candidate artifact, the best validation seed, with
one champion artifact: the interval resamples participants, is
conditional on both fitted models, and carries no training-seed
variation.

\subsection{Calibration effects on directional recall}
\label{sec:maineffects}

\subsection{Mechanism: clipping, not resolution}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} decomposes the sequential-200 loss with the
input-grid controls. Feeding the float model the clip-only image of the
inputs reproduces essentially the entire 13.35-point float-to-INT8
difference (float$-$clip $13.14$; float$-$QDQ $13.12$), so the coarser
input grid itself costs nothing measurable and the remaining
INT8-internal difference is 0.2 points. Under the sequential-200 range, 27.8\% of held-out windows
have at least one clipped input slot by the rounded-code rule ---
60.9\% of \texttt{falling\_fast} windows, the class that collapses ---
against none under the randomized range: 3.5\% of held-out windows
exceed its real-valued top of 400.8~\mgdl\ at the 401 sensor cap, but
those readings round into the top code, so no input slot clips. Wider range coverage therefore did not trade recall
for resolution in this model: the grid-resolution cost of covering
39--401~\mgdl\ was unmeasurable.

\subsection{Delta-encoded inputs}

If calibration failures are input-range failures, re-encoding the input
should remove them. Two variants were trained from scratch on the same
eligible windows and seeds (gates frozen beforehand; like the
sensitivity tiers, this is a post-hoc experiment on previously touched
held-out data): 11 consecutive differences, and the same differences
plus the last absolute value divided by 16, placing a coarse level
channel inside the delta range. Both passed the frozen float gate

reach 0.497, and differences plus the scaled anchor reach
\textbf{0.509} --- above the absolute-input baseline's 0.487, so the

\subsection{Round one: a macro-F1 winner rejected after its read}
\label{sec:sweep1}

Round one asked whether loss design, capacity, window length, pooling
across cohorts or seed ensembling improves the delta-encoded classifier
(\path{protocol_sweep_r1.json}). Stage~A compared four losses at the
study architecture (validation macro-F1, three-seed means). A
distance-penalized CE, this study's own formulation, which adds
$\lambda \sum_j p_j\,|j-y|$ with $\lambda=0.5$ to the weighted CE,
scored 0.427, ahead of weighted CE (0.381), ordinal label smoothing
(also this study's formulation, moving 0.15 of the target mass to the
adjacent classes; 0.390) and focal loss with $\gamma=2$
\cite{lin2017focal} (0.380). Stage~B crossed channel widths with window
length under that loss: (96,144) channels (42,725 parameters) reached
0.439, and a 2-hour window gained at most 0.04 points at any width.
Pooling the Weinstock, dubosson and hall training windows (Stage~C) lost
1.6 points, and a five-seed mean-logit ensemble (Stage~D) did not beat
the best single seed (0.4426 against 0.4430), so seed~1 of the (96,144)
distance-penalized model became the candidate.

The read passed every gate (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): the candidate's INT8
model beat the champion's by $+5.68$ macro-F1 points held-out and
$+5.52$ temporally, within 0.1 point of its own float, and all five
candidate seeds beat the champion (five-seed mean $+5.25$ points). The
candidate was not bundled. A per-class read of the same cached
predictions, made after the gates were scored and not pre-registered
(Table~\ref{tab:sweeprecall}), shows where the gain came from. F1 rose
in every class, but \texttt{stable} contributed $+14.1$ points, half of
the summed per-class gain. Precision rose in all four directional
classes while recall fell in three of them, and stable recall rose from
0.475 to 0.688: the candidate predicts \texttt{stable} more often,
consistent with an operating-point shift rather than better separation
of the directional classes. Directional recall, the quantity the
application reports, fell from 0.517 to 0.438 held-out and from 0.497
to 0.406 temporally. That drop is the basis of the rejection, a decision
taken after the read (Table~\ref{tab:readledger}) that set the endpoint
of every later round. Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r1.json};
per-class precision and F1: \path{panel_revision_r1.json}.

\subsection{Round two: DiaData training under a directional endpoint}

Round two returned to the weighted CE and asked whether more real data,
and how it is mixed, raises directional recall
(\path{protocol_sweep_r2.json}). Stage~M compared five mixtures at
(96,144) channels (three-seed validation means): Weinstock only 0.512;
Weinstock plus the five DiaData training cohorts in natural proportions
0.527 or with equal windows per cohort 0.532; the five DiaData cohorts
alone 0.535; and natural proportions with fast-class oversampling 0.526,
level with natural proportions, as expected once class weights are
recomputed on the oversampled labels. The DiaData-only mixture won with
stable recall
0.540, above the floor; its model never sees a Weinstock training
window. Narrower (24,36) and wider (192,288) channels lost (0.526 and
0.524), and seed~1 led the five seeds at 0.539, against the champion
INT8's 0.519.

The read passed all three gates (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): $+0.60$ points
held-out, stable recall 0.459 against 0.475, and INT8 within 0.11 points
of float; the temporal component agreed in sign. The interval includes
zero, so gate~1 here certifies non-inferiority within 0.5 points, not
superiority. The effect did not hinge on the chosen seed: the five
candidate seeds averaged $+0.55$ points above the champion artifact on
held-out, none below it. Macro-F1 slipped (0.364 against 0.368
held-out), and on PEDAP, which no model trained on (7.0M windows),
directional recall was 0.541 against 0.522. The candidate was bundled
after golden parity passed in the Android unit tests on the Java virtual
machine (JVM), on the iOS simulator and on the iPhone~18 Pro~Max (51
tests, INT8 bit-exact). Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r2.json}.

\subsection{Round three: RT-CGM, time of day and a TCN}

Round three staged three changes against the round-two recipe, five
seeds per arm (\path{protocol_sweep_r3.json}). The data stage
reopened RT-CGM, the JDRF CGM randomized trial \cite{jdrf2008cgm}, which
enrolled children and adults with T1D aged 8 and older on intensive
insulin therapy in 2007 and monitored them with early real-time sensors
(DexCom SEVEN, MiniMed Paradigm REAL-Time or FreeStyle Navigator) after a
blinded run-in. The cohort name denotes this trial, not current
real-time CGM, and DiaData records neither the device nor the wear mode
behind a subject's data (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). The first DiaData
audit had excluded RT-CGM on cadence grounds. A re-audit admitted all 451
subjects (modal within-segment interval 300\,s and $\geq$95\% of
intervals within 270--330\,s; \path{diadata_audit_r2.json}), but that
rule could not fail: DiaData rounds timestamps to a five-minute grid
\cite{cinar2026diadata} and segments split at gaps over 330\,s, so every
within-segment interval is 300\,s by construction. The 96 admitted
subjects without a scored window record every 10 minutes, so no run of
15 consecutive five-minute readings forms; the other 355 subjects supply
the 12.67M windows used in round five. The first audit's collinear
fraction for RT-CGM (0.305, against at most 0.229 for the admitted
cohorts) exceeds theirs mainly through runs of identical readings, which
the statistic cannot tell apart from interpolation.

Adding RT-CGM to training lost 0.6 points of validation directional
recall (five-seed means), and sine and cosine time-of-day input channels
lost 0.3 (timestamps taken as device-local time). The architecture stage
replaced the CNN with a causal dilated temporal convolutional network
(TCN) \cite{bai2018tcn}: four residual blocks, kernel 3, dilations 1--8,
72 channels and 63,005 parameters. It edged the CNN (0.533 against
0.532), and seed~2 led at 0.537.

The TCN's INT8 model beat the champion's by $+0.77$ points held-out, the
first fully positive interval on the directional endpoint, agreed
temporally ($+0.97$) and sat within 0.05 points of its float, but failed
gate~2: stable recall 0.414 against 0.459 held-out ($-4.4$ points against
a 2-point floor; $-3.8$ temporally). Macro-F1 fell (0.341 against
0.364), and on PEDAP directional recall rose slightly (0.545 against
0.541) while stable recall fell (0.361 against 0.394). The candidate was
rejected and the round-two pair stayed bundled. Two selection details
shaped round five. Seed~2 was picked by strict argmax although seed~1
sat 0.01 points behind with higher stable recall (0.536 against 0.526),
and the 5-point selection floor admitted a seed that a 2-point floor
would already have excluded on validation. The $+0.77$ points also
belonged to the chosen seed: across the five candidate seeds the mean
held-out effect was $+0.05$ points, three of five seeds were ahead, and
their float held-out directional recall spanned 1.1 points. Full record:
\path{accuracy_sweep_r3.json}.

\subsection{Round four: a label-definition stability check and an
identity outcome}

Round four took its arms from the champion's error structure, read
descriptively from round three's saved held-out predictions
(Table~\ref{tab:readledger}): 72\% of champion errors were adjacent-class,
and slow-class errors went to the same-direction fast class (47\% of
\texttt{falling} errors, 46\% of \texttt{rising}) more often than to
\texttt{stable} (25\% each). That read chose arms only; selection stayed
on validation.

Before any arm trained, the round compared two labels on the 27,109
validation windows (\path{exp0_sweep_r4.json}). The endpoint label
classifies $(g_{t+15}-g_t)/15$; an alternative label classifies the
least-squares slope over the four readings $g_t,\ldots,g_{t+15}$; both
use the trend-arrow rate bands ($\pm1$ and $\pm2$~\mgdl/min). The slope
label recovers the endpoint label's directional classes with mean recall
0.954 (\texttt{stable} 0.995), and recomputing the endpoint label after
adding independent Gaussian noise of $\sigma=1$ or 2~\mgdl\ to the
readings gives 0.915 and 0.860. This is a label-definition stability
check. Both labels are functions of the same future readings, so their
agreement bounds nothing that a model seeing only the 12 past readings
can reach, and the recall attainable from CGM-only inputs remains
unknown. White noise of this size is also gentler than published CGM
error models, which add blood-to-interstitial lag, calibration drift and
autoregressive noise \cite{facchinetti2014sensor}, and the labels are
defined on the sensor trace, which itself lags blood glucose. The abort
rule, committed in the round's design specification before training,
would have stopped the round had the slope label's agreement exceeded the
champion's validation directional recall (0.533) by less than 2 points;
with both labels sharing the future readings it could hardly fire, and
it did not.

Three stages then varied the objective, the training targets and inputs,
and weight averaging against the round-two recipe at five seeds per arm
(Table~\ref{tab:sweepfour}); every arm lost to the weighted-CE control on
validation directional recall. Square-root class weights lowered it to
0.405 while stable recall rose to 0.827, so the full inverse-frequency
weights, with the slow-to-fast over-prediction they cause, are what buys
directional recall here. A Huber regression head on the rate, decoded at
the same rate bands, collapsed toward \texttt{stable} (0.200 directional,
0.950 stable), the pull toward the dense region of the target that
regression on imbalanced targets is known to suffer
\cite{yang2021imbalanced}. A head for conditional ordinal regression
(CORN) \cite{shi2023corn} reached 0.470. Slope labels for training
changed little ($-0.2$ points), and $\sigma=2$~\mgdl\ input noise traded
directional for stable recall ($-3.7$ points). A uniform average of the
five seeds' weights collapsed (0.238 directional, 0.003 stable), the
expected outcome: weight soups average models fine-tuned from one
pre-trained start \cite{wortsman2022soups}, and independently initialized
runs need not share a linearly connected basin \cite{frankle2020linear}.

The surviving candidate, the control arm's best seed, is the champion's
own checkpoint and converts to SHA-256-identical artifacts
(\texttt{96eef43c\ldots}/\texttt{a887ea4e\ldots}). A contrast would have
compared the model with itself, so no confirmatory read was spent and no
held-out, temporal or PEDAP label was read; the round-two pair stayed
bundled. Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r4.json}.

\subsection{Round five: the seed-4 flatten-head artifact}
\label{sec:sweep5}

Round five tested how the final convolutional features reach the
classifier. The global average pool can erase where in the hour a
movement happened: two synthetic histories carrying the same
10~\mgdl\ rise, placed 30 versus 20 minutes before the last reading,
produce bit-identical outputs from the champion's checkpoint and from
both of its bundled artifacts
(\path{paper/analysis/accuracy-review-2026-10-13/recency_collision.json}), an existence proof rather than a rate
on real data. A recipe stage compared the round-two recipe with the
cosine schedule and validation-chosen kept epoch of
Section~\ref{sec:sweepprotocol}; a head stage then replaced the pool
with a flatten head, in which the $144\times12$ feature map feeds a
1,728-to-5 linear layer (50,645 parameters against 42,725), under the
winning recipe; a final stage picked the seed
(\path{protocol_sweep_r5.json}). Selection took the cosine recipe
(five-seed validation means 0.535 against 0.532), then the flatten head
(0.538 against 0.535), and seed~4 led at 0.541 directional and 0.546
stable recall. Seed~4 is the only flatten-head seed above the champion
checkpoint's own validation float (0.539), and these numbers are
selection-biased: the kept epoch and the seed were chosen on the split
they are quoted from. The candidate differs from the champion in head,
learning-rate schedule, kept epoch and seed at once, and its INT8
artifact is calibrated with uniform-200 from the Weinstock validation
pool, a cohort it never trained on.

The read moved to RT-CGM, whose labels no round had evaluated: 12.67M
eligible windows from the 355 of 451 admitted subjects with scored
windows. Round three had trained a rejected arm on this cohort, but
neither model under comparison trained on it, so the comparison is
zero-shot for both. All three gates passed (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): the
candidate's INT8 model beat the champion's by $+0.20$ directional-recall
points [$+0.11$, $+0.29$], held stable recall within 0.2 points (0.605
against 0.607) and sat 0.25 points under its own float. The candidate
replaced the round-two pair under the rule fixed before the read, but the
result is a choice between two artifacts, not evidence for a design. The
$+0.20$ points is a fifth of the 1.1-point spread of round
three's five seeds, and round three's chosen-seed effect ($+0.77$)
shrank to $+0.05$ at the five-seed mean; the five-seed RT-CGM comparison
of the two recipes, which would measure that variation here, was not
run. The four changed factors are not separately identified. The
non-gating secondaries carry costs: on both Weinstock components the
candidate fails gates~1 and~2 (held-out $-0.32$ points with stable
recall 0.436 against 0.459; temporal $+0.33$ with 0.499 against 0.520),
macro-F1 is lower on all three paired splits, including RT-CGM (0.410
against 0.427), and PEDAP directional recall is 0.539 against 0.541.
PEDAP's per-class changes show where the predictions moved:
\texttt{falling\_fast} recall rose 6.7 points and \texttt{rising\_fast}
2.3, while \texttt{falling} fell 6.5, \texttt{rising} 3.1 and
\texttt{stable} 0.3. The candidate labels 16.0\% of PEDAP windows
\texttt{falling\_fast} against the champion's 12.3\%, and its
\texttt{falling\_fast} precision drops from 0.284 to 0.242, consistent
with an operating-point shift from the slow to the fast classes rather
than better discrimination. Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r5.json};
PEDAP per-class detail: \path{panel_revision_r1.json}.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What nominal sample count hides}
\label{sec:calibevidence}

Official guidance asks for a small representative calibration set
\cite{googleRepresentativeData,googleLiteRTQuantization}, and prior PTQ work
treats clipping, range estimation, and rounding as the levers
\cite{banner2019ptq,nagel2020adaround,hubara2021smallcalibration}. This
study's sharpest observation is that \emph{nominal sample count said
nothing}: the sequential-200 and uniform-200 sets have identical size and
differ by 13.4 points of held-out directional recall, while the 200- and
1,000-window uniform sets differ by nothing. What mattered was measured
coverage --- participants spanned and, through them, the input range.
Overlapping sliding windows make this failure easy to produce: the
toolchain-default pattern of taking the first $N$ windows concentrates an
entire calibration set inside a few hours of one or two sensors.

Once the selection is randomized at all, the refinements evaluated here
are exhausted. Stratifying by input-range cells on top of participant
balancing --- the prespecified primary contrast --- moves the endpoint by
six hundredths of a point. The histogram and min--max observers agree.
The feared resolution trade-off, where covering 39--401~\mgdl\ coarsens
the grid by 60\%, costs nothing measurable, consistent with a label that
depends on the difference between readings rather than their absolute
code. For this model class, calibration design reduces to one rule:
do not let contiguous window order pick your calibration data.

The mechanism controls localize the sequential-arm error at the input
edge: clipping alone reproduces the loss, the quantized grid without
clipping is harmless, and the INT8 interior contributes a quarter point.
The class asymmetry follows directly --- 61\% of \texttt{falling\_fast}
windows contain a reading above the sequential range, since fast falls
start high. A model that cannot see how high the trajectory started
cannot recognize a fast fall.

\subsection{Scope of the accuracy illusion}

The sequential-arm INT8 model is a worse clinical-shape classifier with
better accuracy (0.570 vs.\ 0.507): clipping pushes predictions toward
\texttt{stable}, the class holding three quarters of the weight.
Any pipeline that gates a quantized model on raw accuracy would have
promoted the broken artifact. Directional (minority-class) recall, or any
class-balanced metric, exposes it immediately
\cite{brodersen2010balanced}. This is the practical reading of the study
for small imbalanced time-series deployments.

Parity and runtime agreement are necessary but orthogonal checks: the
Python, Kotlin, and Swift clients agree bit-exactly on dequantized INT8
outputs for the tested vectors, yet parity says nothing about whether the
artifact's input range fits the population, and validates neither the
labels, the cohort split, probability calibration, clinical safety, nor
usefulness to a person with diabetes.

\subsection{What the sweeps show}
\label{sec:sweepdiscussion}

The sweep program's main lesson is that a pre-registered endpoint can
pass every gate and still optimize the wrong thing. Round one's F1 rose
in every class while directional recall, the quantity the application
reports, fell by 7.9 points; pre-registration fixed how the endpoint was
analyzed, not whether it was the right endpoint. From round two on, the
gates encode that quantity, directional recall under a stable-recall
floor, but the floor is relative to each round's champion. Held-out
stable recall therefore drifted from 0.475 to 0.459 to 0.436 across the
two promotions, the second gated on another cohort; a floor anchored to
a fixed reference would prevent that drift.

What the rounds established is narrower than five rounds of selection
suggest. Round one's gain held at every seed (five-seed mean $+5.25$
macro-F1 points) and lay on the wrong axis. Round two's DiaData-only
recipe matched or beat the delta-encoded champion artifact at every seed
on held-out (mean $+0.55$ points), the best-supported gain of the program,
though on a reused split and with a confirmatory interval that includes
zero. Round three showed how much seed choice alone can contribute:
$+0.77$ points for the chosen seed, $+0.05$ at the five-seed mean. Round
four's arms all lost on validation, two of them in ways prior work
predicts. Round five showed that one artifact passed the pre-registered
gates on a cohort no round had evaluated, by $+0.20$ points against a
1.1-point five-seed spread, while failing gates~1 and~2 on both Weinstock
components and lowering macro-F1 on RT-CGM (0.410 against 0.427). It did
not show that a flatten head, a cosine
schedule or validation-chosen epochs improve this task: the four factors
changed together, one seed per side was compared, and the five-seed
RT-CGM comparison was not run. No round measured the recall attainable
from 55 minutes of CGM history.

A displayed class is the model's estimate of the rate band that the
sensor-reported glucose will average over the next 15 minutes. It is
neither a measurement of the current rate nor a reading of blood
glucose. On both evaluated cohorts (Table~\ref{tab:shippederrors}),
about three in five displayed movement classes were followed by a mean
rate within $\pm1$~\mgdl/min; a displayed \texttt{falling\_fast} was
followed by a falling or fast-falling rate in a third (held-out) to a
half (RT-CGM) of windows; 9--15\% of truly falling or rising windows
were shown in the opposite direction; and movement was recognized least
often below 70~\mgdl. The fast classes trade PPV for sensitivity, the
operating point that inverse-frequency class weights and a
directional-recall endpoint select (Table~\ref{tab:sweepfour}). These
figures describe a research classifier on retrospective data; they are
not evidence about how a person would use its output.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The results should be read with the following limitations in mind.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item This is a locked, corrected reanalysis, not a fresh test. The
    held-out participants were scored once before under the flawed
    aggregate protocol, and the five-class decision was informed by a
    historical test run. The protocol, samplers, and endpoint were frozen
    before the new held-out labels were read, but prior exposure cannot be
    undone by re-splitting \cite{kapoor2023leakage}.
    \item One architecture, one quantizer, and one primary cohort. The
    source cohort comprised older adults with long-standing type-1
    diabetes and blinded CGM collection \cite{weinstock2016risk}. The
    sequential-versus-uniform effect was confirmed on two further
    cohorts with prespecified paired predictions
    (Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}), but all other contrasts remain
    single-cohort \cite{ghimire2024generalize}, and the null results for range
    stratification, budget, and observer are statements about this
    2,909-parameter CNN with per-tensor affine input quantization, not
    about PTQ in general. Larger models, other datasets, or other
    quantizers may behave differently
    \cite{bouthillier2021variance,hubara2021smallcalibration}.
    \item The accuracy sweeps reuse evaluation data. Weinstock held-out
    and temporal labels were read in rounds one to three, so round five
    demoted them to descriptive secondaries and moved its primary
    endpoint to RT-CGM, which no round had evaluated; a rejected
    round-three arm had trained on that cohort, though neither model in
    the round-five comparison did. The certified round-five gain is small
    ($+0.20$ points) and endpoint-shaped: macro-F1 and secondary stable
    recall fall, the winner is a single seed, and its validation numbers
    are selection-biased.
    \item Stride-one windows remain overlapping and dependent
    \cite{bergmeir2018crossvalidation}; uncertainty therefore comes from
    participant-cluster bootstrap intervals conditional on the fitted
    models and calibration sets, with model-seed and draw variation
    averaged inside each bootstrap draw rather than treated as
    independent participants.
    \item The gap-fill mask removes windows around missing readings, but
    the retained grid still contains formatter re-gridding between
    adjacent readings, and interpolation precedes windowing, so
    retrospective preprocessing may use information unavailable in real
    time. The three frozen sensitivity tiers (exact-observed windows;
    gap-filled inputs with gap-free endpoints; any gap-filled endpoint)
    reproduce both headline findings
    (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}), but the tier evaluation ran after the
    primary analysis was published, and the gap-filled-endpoint tier's
    labels score agreement with the formatter's interpolated grid, not
    with unobserved sensor readings.
    \item The five labels are deterministic transformations of a future
    CGM point, not expert annotations, and the CGM-only model omits meal,
    insulin, activity, illness, and other covariates. Probability
    calibration and event-level performance were not evaluated.
    \item The nine prespecified artifacts were device-timed on one
    iPhone (Table~\ref{tab:devicebench}); repeated calls on one phone
    are not evidence across devices, the historical Android device is
    no longer available, initialization was measured within-process,
    and energy and memory were not measured. The remaining 101 matrix,
    confirmation-cohort, and delta-encoded artifacts were not
    device-timed but share one operator inventory and byte size with
    the timed ones; earlier bundled pairs (delta-r1 and round-two)
    passed on-device parity, with single live in-app sessions (100
    inferences per model) observing means between 0.080 and 0.149~ms
    --- observations, not paired-protocol measurements. The round-five
    pair bundled at submission passed the simulator and JVM parity
    suites bit-exactly, the same suite passed on the iPhone 18 Pro
    Max on ship day (51 tests, golden parity included), and a live
    in-app session the same day observed float 0.124~ms mean /
    0.182~ms p95 and INT8 0.126~ms / 0.179~ms --- again observations,
    not paired-protocol measurements.
    \item The application replays synthetic or locally extracted
    historical traces. It does not ingest a live sensor stream and has
    not undergone prospective, external, usability, or clinical
    validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reproducibility and Ethics}
\label{sec:repro}

\subsection{Code, artifacts, and computational record}

The repository contains the training, conversion, benchmark, Android, iOS,
and test code. The bundled artifacts are the round-five sweep winner
(seed 4), whose confirmatory protocol was frozen at repository commit
\texttt{f9010b6} before the read. Their SHA-256 digests are \texttt{bfb37468...6153}
(float) and \texttt{eacc0a74...f554} (INT8); full hashes are stored beside
the assets and asserted by the golden-parity tests. Python unit
tests cover label boundaries, segment-aware windows, model shape, conversion
helpers, synthetic replay behavior, and the provenance audit's split
bookkeeping. Android unit tests cover replay, buffering, quantization,
latency aggregation, and view-model behavior; a merged-manifest check
enforces the absence of Internet permission. The iOS suite covers the
same units plus golden-vector parity, run in CI on a simulator with a
source scan for networking APIs.

The conversion requirements record LiteRT Torch 0.9.1, TorchAO 0.17.0, and
Python LiteRT 2.1.5, and document PyTorch 2.12.1 and NumPy 2.5.0 in the
historical environment (the top-level requirements pinned older versions);
these records are not a complete environment lock. PyTorch seed 0 and the
formatter's split seed 0 control separate randomization steps; neither
guarantees bitwise retraining across platforms. The artifact hashes identify
the exact models used for replay.

The September revision reused the July results and computed descriptive
summaries from the rounded tables; the subsequent provenance audit reran only
the GlucoBench formatter and window construction; and the October revision
added the iOS client and its device measurements (10 October 2026, unchanged
artifacts, same SHA-256 hashes) --- none of these retrained, re-evaluated, or
touched held-out glucose values. The audit's aggregate output, including the

as \texttt{paper/results/split\_audit.json}; machine-readable run reports and

The calibration-coverage study is versioned under

PyTorch 2.12.1, NumPy 2.5.3, reconciling the previously recorded

the bundled application artifacts were replaced by its seed-0 float and

\section{Conclusion}

On a corrected, provenance-masked protocol with five training seeds and
100 paired INT8 artifacts, calibration-data coverage fully determined
whether static INT8 quantization damaged this compact CGM trend
classifier. The toolchain-default sequential selection, whose 200
windows span two participants and cap the input range at 251~\mgdl,
cost 13.4 points of held-out directional recall while raising raw
accuracy --- an illusion a class-balanced metric exposes. Any
randomized selection of the same 200 windows restored float-level
directional recall; stratification, budget, and observer choice moved
it by at most eight hundredths of a point; clip-only controls
attributed the loss to input-range clipping, not grid resolution.
Every INT8 artifact is byte-identical in size and selection takes
milliseconds: the corrected calibration is free at deployment time.
Coverage means covering participants; the other knobs were the wrong
ones.

The five accuracy sweeps that followed add a methodological lesson. Each
round spent one pre-registered confirmatory read, and gates alone were
not enough: round one's macro-F1 winner passed every gate while losing
directional recall, which made a directional endpoint and a stable-recall
floor part of every later protocol. Under those rules two changes were
promoted, external training data (DiaData) and a position-preserving
head with a cosine schedule; a TCN, time-of-day inputs, class reweighting, ordinal and regression
heads, slope labels, input noise, and weight averaging did not, and a
measured label-noise ceiling far above the champion places the remaining
error in the model and data, not the labels. The round-five model,
confirmed zero-shot on a cohort with no prior reads, ships in both
applications; its gain is small and is reported with its costs.

The calibration conclusions are scoped to one primary cohort, one
architecture, and one quantizer, on a reanalysis of previously touched data; the frozen
sensitivity tiers reproduce both headline findings, the device
benchmark measured the fix latency-free, and the
sequential-versus-uniform effect was confirmed on two further cohorts
with prespecified paired predictions tracking the calibration-coverage
deficit. \project remains a research prototype, not a medical device.
