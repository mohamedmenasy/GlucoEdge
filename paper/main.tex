\begin{abstract}
How does calibration-data coverage affect minority-class performance and
deployment cost in a compact, quantized glucose trend classifier? We answer
this with a controlled study on \project, an open-source Android and iOS
prototype that maps 12 five-minute continuous glucose monitor (CGM) readings
to one of five rate-of-change classes 15 minutes ahead. On a corrected
evaluation protocol --- split-role provenance masks, gap-free windows, and a
separate held-out-participant component --- we trained a 2,909-parameter CNN
at five seeds on public Weinstock data prepared by GlucoBench, then
quantized each checkpoint with four calibration-selection strategies at two
budgets and three draws: 100 INT8 artifacts, evaluated pairwise against
their float sources. The toolchain-default sequential selection reproduces
the failure we previously shipped: the first eligible windows cover two
participants, cap the INT8 input range at 251~\mgdl, and cost 13.4
percentage points of held-out directional recall (95\% CI 10.3--16.9)
relative to a uniform random selection of the same size, while raw accuracy
rises --- an illusion created by the majority class. Any randomized
200-window selection restores float-level directional recall (0.487 vs.\
0.487); range-aware stratification adds nothing further (+0.06 points, CI
$-0.02$ to $+0.13$), a five-fold calibration budget changes it by at
most $+0.08$ points, and a
min--max observer behaves like the default histogram observer. Clip-only
controls attribute the entire sequential-arm loss to input-range clipping
rather than grid resolution, and every INT8 artifact is byte-identical in
size, so the fix costs nothing at deployment time: calibration coverage
here means covering participants, not tuning budgets or samplers. The CNN
outperforms recent-slope and logistic-regression baselines on directional
recall. Prior single-device latency observations are reported as historical
context; the new artifacts have not been device-timed. \project is a
research demonstration, not a medical device or treatment system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A CGM sampled every five minutes produces 12 observations per hour. These
readings can be used to predict a near-future glucose value, event, or trend
\cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}. Here, we study such predictions for research
purposes; they are not intended to guide treatment.

These targets define different learning problems. Value regression estimates a
future glucose level, event prediction asks whether a glycemic event will occur,
and trend classification assigns a directional rate category. Published studies
also differ in prediction horizon, use of contextual inputs, cohort,
split construction, and metric. Reviews and comparison indices document these
differences. Public benchmarks and cross-dataset experiments help distinguish
performance within a dataset from transfer to another data source
\cite{liu2023systematic,facchinetti2011index,
sergazinov2024glucobench,ghimire2024generalize}. Scores from incompatible
protocols should therefore not be compared directly.

Commercial CGM trend arrows are related but not equivalent targets. They
summarize recent or current glucose direction and rate using device-specific
definitions \cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend}. In contrast,
\project derives each label from the change between the current sample and the
sample 15 minutes later. The arrow-like rate bands provide a compact research
representation, but the future-derived label neither reproduces a manufacturer's
algorithm nor provides a dosing rule.

Deployment raises further questions. Conversion may change a model's outputs,
and the client may need different input handling. Lower precision also does not
guarantee lower latency on a given device. Class imbalance can hide conversion
errors:
accuracy can increase as predictions concentrate on a majority class even while
minority-class recall declines \cite{brodersen2010balanced}.

The research question is: \emph{how does calibration-data coverage affect
minority-class performance and deployment cost in compact glucose trend
classifiers?} Here, coverage refers to the input values and trajectories
represented during quantizer calibration, not calibration of predicted
probabilities. An earlier revision of this work could only motivate the
question: a provenance audit traced the shipped INT8 artifact's input range
to a single participant's 17.5 hours of data, whose maximum set a ceiling
below readings found in a quarter of training windows, but no comparison of
calibration selections existed. This revision runs that comparison under a
prespecified protocol, frozen --- hypotheses, samplers, endpoint,
missing-class rules, and environment --- before any held-out label was read.

The study holds the architecture, loss, quantizer, and data split fixed and
varies only calibration selection: four strategies (the toolchain-default
sequential order, uniform random, participant-balanced random, and
participant-balanced range coverage), two budgets (200 and 1,000 windows,
nested), three random draws, and five training seeds, giving 100 INT8
artifacts evaluated against their five float sources, plus a ten-artifact
min--max-observer arm. The evaluation protocol is corrected relative to the
earlier revision: split-role provenance masks remove context-only targets,
a gap-fill mask removes windows touched by interpolation of missing
readings, and held-out-participant evaluation is separated from temporal
evaluation on training participants. This paper reports:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a corrected, provenance-masked evaluation protocol for
    GlucoBench-formatted CGM windows, with eligible-window and class-support
    audits published alongside the code;
    \item a controlled answer to the coverage question: randomizing a
    200-window calibration selection recovers all of the directional recall
    the default sequential selection loses (13.4 points, 95\% CI
    10.3--16.9), while range-aware stratification, a five-fold budget, and
    an observer change add nothing measurable;
    \item mechanism controls that attribute the sequential-arm loss to
    input-range clipping rather than input-grid resolution or internal
    quantization; and
    \item deployment-cost accounting: every INT8 artifact has identical
    size, sampler and export times are fractions of a second, so the
    corrected calibration is free at deployment time. Prior
    single-device latency observations are retained as historical context.
\end{itemize}

All source code, tests, synthetic replay data, and shipped model artifacts are
available in the project repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/mohamedmenasy/GlucoEdge}}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Forecasting tasks, horizons, and inputs}

CGM forecasting has expanded from autoregressive value prediction using sensor
history \cite{reifman2007predictive} to recurrent and attention models trained
with CGM and contextual variables \cite{mirshekarian2019lstm}. Recurrent models
have also been used to estimate predictive variance rather than only a point
value \cite{martinsson2020variance}, while convolutional--recurrent models fuse
CGM, insulin, and meal sequences before predicting 30- or 60-minute values
\cite{li2020crnn}. Architecture and input modality change together across these
studies, so their results do not isolate the effect of model family alone.
Cross-dataset evaluation asks a different question: whether a trained model
generalizes when the source dataset, cohort, and collection process change
\cite{ghimire2024generalize}.

Table~\ref{tab:relatedwork} summarizes these design differences. It distinguishes
offline predictive evaluation from reported device execution and omits scores
that would be misleading to compare across protocols.

\subsection{Benchmarks and evaluation protocols}

The split determines what a study can claim about generalization.
Subject-dependent splits evaluate people represented during training;
held-out-participant splits evaluate unseen people within the same dataset.
Cross-dataset evaluation also changes the cohort and data pipeline. GlucoBench
standardizes preprocessing, splits, and baselines across five public datasets
\cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}; experiments across four datasets directly test
changes in data source \cite{ghimire2024generalize}.

\project uses GlucoBench's largest type-1 diabetes cohort, referred to as
Weinstock. Its source study collected blinded CGM from older adults with
long-standing type-1 diabetes \cite{weinstock2016risk}.

Time-series splitting also requires care because nearby and overlapping windows
are dependent. The validity of cross-validation for autoregressive prediction
depends on the residual structure rather than a generic random-split rule
\cite{bergmeir2018crossvalidation}, and leakage can produce answers to a less
demanding problem than the one a study claims to test
\cite{kapoor2023leakage}. Even with a fixed split, training and implementation
choices contribute benchmark variance \cite{bouthillier2021variance}. Results
therefore need to state the participant, temporal, and dataset boundaries used
in evaluation.

\subsection{Trend representations and class imbalance}

Commercial trend arrows summarize recent or current movement using
device-specific categories whose rate bands and look-back intervals differ
\cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend}. A future label instead asks
whether the trajectory after the input window falls, remains stable, or rises.
\project uses these rate bands to define a research target from the sample
15 minutes after the window. It does not reproduce a commercial arrow
algorithm or support treatment decisions.

In the Weinstock windows used here, most labels are stable and faster changes
are relatively rare. Overall accuracy therefore weights the stable class in
proportion to its prevalence. Macro-averaged recall, equivalent to balanced
accuracy for this single-label setting, gives each class equal weight and makes
minority-class degradation visible \cite{brodersen2010balanced}. Macro recall
still does not establish event-level or clinical performance.

\subsection{Edge inference and quantization}

One-dimensional CNNs extract local temporal features with compact feed-forward
networks \cite{kiranyaz2021survey}.

Mobile inference systems can reduce neural-network execution cost through
compression and runtime-specific scheduling \cite{lane2016deepx}, but latency is
a property of the model, runtime, and hardware together. Li et al. reported
Android timing for a glucose forecasting model \cite{li2020crnn}; broader
smartphone and tiny-device benchmarks also measure specified workloads and
platforms because model size alone does not determine speed
\cite{ignatov2019aibenchmark,banbury2021mlperftiny}.

Integer quantization approximates floating-point tensors with discrete values
parameterized by a scale and zero point \cite{jacob2018quantization}. Static
post-training quantization estimates activation ranges from calibration inputs
before conversion \cite{pytorchPT2E2026,googleRepresentativeData}. Low-bit
studies show that clipping, range estimation, and rounding choices can
determine the resulting error \cite{banner2019ptq,nagel2020adaround};
specialized methods have also been proposed to improve post-training accuracy
when calibration sets are small \cite{hubara2021smallcalibration}. These
findings support checking converted outputs and per-class metrics alongside
model size.

\project connects public preprocessing with conversion parity, class-wise
evaluation, and offline replay on one Android and one iOS device; it does not
establish live-sensor, clinical, or cross-hardware performance.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $g_t$ be the CGM value at time $t$, in \mgdl, with observations every five
minutes. The input is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t = [g_{t-55}, g_{t-50}, \ldots, g_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{12}.
\end{equation}
The future rate of change is computed from a point outside the input window:
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \frac{g_{t+15}-g_t}{15}\quad [\mgdl/\mathrm{min}].
\end{equation}
The class $y_t$ is assigned by
\begin{equation}
y_t =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{falling\_fast}, & r_t \leq -2,\\
\texttt{falling},       & -2 < r_t \leq -1,\\
\texttt{stable},        & -1 < r_t < 1,\\
\texttt{rising},        & 1 \leq r_t < 2,\\
\texttt{rising\_fast}, & r_t \geq 2.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The 12 readings span 55 elapsed minutes; ``one-hour history'' refers to 12
samples at a five-minute cadence. The history length and 15-minute horizon
are fixed engineering choices for this prototype. The five-class target is a
simplification based on two samples and does not describe the full intervening
trajectory.
The boundaries use rate bands commonly represented by CGM trend arrows, while
collapsing device-specific arrow taxonomies into five classes
\cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend}. No threshold was optimized on
the test labels.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset and preprocessing}

The experiments use the Weinstock data distributed through GlucoBench. The
source cohort comprised older adults with long-standing type-1 diabetes and
blinded CGM collection \cite{weinstock2016risk}. GlucoBench reports 200
participants before preprocessing and 192 afterwards, with a five-minute
sampling interval \cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}; our audit of the formatter
output reproduces both counts. The configuration used here disables scaling
and retains glucose in raw \mgdl. The formatter rounds timestamps to the
minute, splits each participant's record at gaps longer than 45 minutes, drops
segments with fewer than 240 original readings, and linearly interpolates each
remaining segment onto a five-minute grid that starts at its first reading.
Each segment is tagged with an \texttt{id\_segment}. Of the 579,782 grid
values, 75.8\% coincide with an original reading. A further 19.8\% fall
between two consecutive readings no more than 7.5 minutes apart, because
reading times drift relative to the grid; 4.4\% fill one or more missing
readings. Because interpolation precedes windowing, interpolated values may
occur among the inputs or at samples used to form targets. The configured
split holds out 10\% of participants (20 people) for
an out-of-distribution component and forms temporal validation and test
components from the remaining participants. The formatter combines the test
components into one frame; Section~\ref{sec:eligible} separates them again
by row ownership, and all results report the held-out-participant and
temporal components separately.

Windows are generated independently within each $(\texttt{id},
\texttt{id\_segment})$ group and ordered by time. A window is emitted only when
all 12 input points and the three following horizon steps fit in the same
segment. The stride is one reading, matching an application that updates every
five minutes. This rule prevents a sensor gap or participant boundary from
being treated as a continuous trajectory. Adjacent stride-one windows overlap
heavily and are statistically dependent. The resulting frames yielded 424,462 training,
55,289 validation, and 127,165 aggregate test windows from 172, 127, and 186
participants, respectively. The test windows comprise 57,068 from the 20
held-out participants and 70,097 from temporal segments of 166 training
participants.

Table~\ref{tab:distribution} shows the resulting class imbalance. Both fast
classes exceed 13,000 training windows. A preliminary 20-epoch run obtained
test recalls of 0.5333 and 0.5380 for \texttt{falling\_fast} and
\texttt{rising\_fast}, respectively. Neither met the predeclared condition for
collapsing the classes: fewer than 200 training examples or recall below 0.30.
The deployed model therefore retains all five classes. Test recall informed
this five-versus-three decision, so it must be treated as an exploratory
development choice rather than independent validation.

\subsection{Split roles, observation mask, and eligible windows}
\label{sec:eligible}

GlucoBench repeats rows from a preceding split at the top of validation and
in-distribution test frames so sequence models can form initial histories.
The earlier revision of this work let every prefix row start a window, so
13.6\% of aggregate test windows scored targets in validation-owned rows and
35.1\% of validation windows duplicated training windows
(Table~\ref{tab:provenance}). The corrected protocol assigns every formatter
row to the earliest split that lists it and scores a window in a split only
when its anchor (last input sample) and all three forecast rows are owned by
that split. Earlier-split readings may serve as causal history among the
inputs. Scored $(\text{participant}, \text{segment}, \text{target time})$
keys are asserted disjoint across splits.

Because the formatter interpolates before windowing, a second mask removes
gap filling: a grid value is retained only if it lies on or between two
original readings at most 7.5 minutes apart. An audit showed that requiring
exact coincidence with an original reading would discard roughly 40\% of
windows for grid drift unrelated to missing data, so the gap-fill rule is
the primary mask and exact coincidence is a sensitivity tier. A window is
eligible when its 12 inputs and future endpoint are all gap-free; the two
intermediate forecast steps are not label inputs and are not masked.
Table~\ref{tab:eligible} shows the result. Eligible training windows retain
all five classes with at least 10,330 examples
(\texttt{falling\_fast}); the calibration pool keeps 127 of 172 development
participants.

\subsection{Model and training}

The \texttt{TrendCNN} accepts tensors of shape $(B,1,12)$. It applies a
one-dimensional convolution from 1 to 24 channels, a second convolution from
24 to 36 channels, ReLU after each layer, adaptive average pooling over time,
and a 36-to-5 linear head. Both convolutions use kernel size 3, stride 1, and
padding 1. The network contains 2,909 trainable parameters.

Training uses inverse-frequency class-weighted cross-entropy, batch size 64,
and Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 20 epochs
\cite{kingma2015adam,paszke2019pytorch}. The study trains five checkpoints
with seeds 0--4 on the eligible windows of Section~\ref{sec:eligible}; the
fixed split does not change with the training seed. Validation loss was
observed after every epoch; there was no early stopping or test-time
selection. Classification metrics were computed with scikit-learn and the
study's own confusion-count code \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. Because the
test distribution is dominated by \texttt{stable}, the primary summary is
the unweighted mean recall of the four directional classes (minority macro
recall); five-class macro recall, equivalent to balanced accuracy, is also
reported \cite{brodersen2010balanced}.

Two input-dependent baselines use the same eligible windows. The recent-slope
baseline maps $(g_t - g_{t-15})/15$, computed from the inputs alone, through
the same five thresholds. The logistic-regression baseline is a multinomial
model on the 12 readings with training-only standardization, balanced class
weights, and fixed $C=1$; it converged. The constant-\texttt{stable}
classifier shows the effect of prevalence. The shipped application artifact
(seed 0, historical protocol) is unchanged; no new artifact replaces it in
the applications.

\section{Conversion and Deployment}

\subsection{Shipped artifacts (historical protocol)}

The shipped application checkpoint was converted with LiteRT Torch. Float conversion uses a
fixed $(1,1,12)$ input signature. INT8 conversion uses PyTorch 2 Export (PT2E)
with LiteRT Torch's \texttt{get\_symmetric\_quantization\_config} helper
(\texttt{is\_per\_channel=True}, \texttt{is\_dynamic=False}). Despite the
helper's name, only the weights are quantized symmetrically, per channel.
Activations, including the model input, use per-tensor affine quantization
whose ranges a histogram observer selects to minimize quantization error
\cite{pytorchPT2E2026}. The first 200 validation windows serve as calibration
inputs. Selection follows the dataset's participant/segment and time order,
with no randomization or attempt to balance input-range coverage;
Section~\ref{sec:provenance} shows that all 200 windows come from one
participant. The converted model's input scale is 0.9408~\mgdl\ per code with
zero point $-128$, so its representable input range is 0--239.9~\mgdl. The
range starts at zero because the observer always includes zero, which leaves
the codes below the lowest CGM reading unused.

\subsection{Calibration experiment design}
\label{sec:design}

The study freezes the checkpoint, quantizer, and conversion pipeline and
varies only which validation windows calibrate the histogram observer. The
calibration pool is the 27,109 eligible validation windows; no sampler
reads future labels, and selection code is tested to be invariant under
label shuffling. Four strategies are compared. \emph{Sequential} takes the
first windows in deterministic (participant, segment, time) order --- the
toolchain-default pattern that produced the shipped artifact.
\emph{Uniform random} is a seeded permutation of the pool.
\emph{Participant-balanced random} fixes a shuffled participant order per
draw and cycles it round-robin, taking one window from a fixed
per-participant permutation each visit and skipping exhausted participants.
\emph{Participant-balanced range coverage} reuses exactly the same
participant allocation but, within each participant, cycles through
low/middle/high range cells defined by the pool's own 10th and 90th
percentiles of per-window input minimum and maximum, so it differs from the
balanced-random arm only in within-participant input-range coverage.

Each strategy is drawn at budgets of 200 and 1,000 windows, with the
200-window set nested as the first 200 of the 1,000-window ordering.
Stochastic strategies use three seeded draws (101--103); all selections are
shared across the five model seeds. This yields 100 INT8 artifacts (5 seeds
$\times$ [2 sequential + 3 strategies $\times$ 2 budgets $\times$ 3 draws]).
A mechanism-control arm re-quantizes two cells (sequential-200 and
range-coverage-200, draw 101) with a min--max activation observer in place
of the histogram observer, adding 10 artifacts. For every calibration set
the study records participants, segments, unique input readings, input
extremes and quantiles, and occupied range cells, because a nominal window
count can cover few distinct readings; for every artifact it records input
quantization parameters, size, and SHA-256.

\subsection{Client implementations}

For input scale $s$ and zero point $z$, both clients use the affine form
\begin{equation}
    q = \operatorname{clip}\left(\operatorname{round}(x/s)+z,
    -128,127\right), \qquad \hat{x}=s(q-z).
\end{equation}
Quantization parameters are read from the model metadata rather than hardcoded.
All three clients divide in single precision and round exact half-way values
to the even integer: Python with \texttt{numpy.round}, Kotlin with
\texttt{kotlin.math.round}, and Swift with
\texttt{rounded(.toNearestOrEven)} \cite{numpyRound,kotlinRound,swiftRounding}.
They therefore produce identical integer inputs for any glucose value. The July device tests ran an
earlier Kotlin version that rounded ties toward positive infinity. The
golden-vector inputs are given to 0.1~\mgdl, and none of those values is a
tie at the shipped input scale, so the expected outputs are unchanged.

The Kotlin/Jetpack Compose application replays a bundled synthetic trace. A
12-reading ring buffer resets after a cadence gap, mirroring training-time
segment boundaries. Every complete window is classified locally; the merged
Android manifest is tested to ensure it does not request the
\texttt{INTERNET} permission. The float artifact is the default and INT8 can be
selected for comparison.

Real devices use LiteRT 2.1.0 \texttt{CompiledModel} on CPU
\cite{googleLiteRTCompiledModel}. On the tested
Apple-Silicon-hosted Android emulator, native model creation terminated with an
illegal-instruction fault during a CPU feature probe for the Arm Scalable
Matrix Extension (SME). Because this fault
cannot be caught by Kotlin, emulators are selected before runtime initialization
and use the classic interpreter with XNNPACK disabled. This workaround applies
to the tested environment; it does not establish performance across LiteRT
configurations.

A SwiftUI iOS application mirrors the Android client. It bundles the same two
artifact files and synthetic trace by reference to the Android asset
directory, so both platforms ship byte-identical models. It runs them through
LiteRT's Swift \texttt{CompiledModel} API on CPU, using the LiteRT 2.3.0
release binaries. Replay, the gap-resetting window buffer, the model switch,
and the latency statistics follow the Kotlin implementation. iOS has no
equivalent of Android's \texttt{INTERNET} permission. Instead, a source scan
fails the build's continuous-integration job if networking APIs appear in the
application code. This guard is weaker than the Android manifest check.

\section{Experimental Protocol}

Evaluation uses two disjoint components from Section~\ref{sec:eligible}:
the 46,376 eligible windows of the 20 held-out participants (primary) and
the 37,361 eligible temporal windows of 166 training participants
(secondary). They are never concatenated. The protocol --- hypotheses,
samplers, budgets, endpoint, bootstrap, and missing-class rules --- was
frozen and committed before any held-out label was read, in
\texttt{paper/results/} \texttt{calibration-coverage/protocol.json}. Because an
earlier revision of this work evaluated on an overlapping aggregate frame
and the five-class decision was informed by a historical test run, the
study is a locked, corrected reanalysis of previously touched data, not a
fresh test set.

A provenance audit (\texttt{experiments/split\_audit.py}) maps every window
to the formatter rows it uses and assigns each row to the earliest split
that lists it. It reads row identities and timestamps for all splits but
glucose values only from training and validation rows. It also records the
composition of the shipped calibration set and the shipped INT8 model's
input quantization parameters.

If $n_c$ is class support, $N=\sum_c n_c$, and $R_c$ is class recall, then
\begin{equation}
    A=\sum_{c=1}^{5}\frac{n_c}{N}R_c,
    \qquad R_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{c=1}^{5}R_c,
\end{equation}
and the primary endpoint is the unweighted mean recall of the four
directional classes. The prespecified primary contrast is range-coverage
versus participant-balanced random at budget 200, which isolates
within-participant input-range coverage from participant representation.
Secondary contrasts are range coverage versus uniform random, 1,000 versus
200 within each strategy, and uniform random versus sequential at each
budget. A recall is undefined on zero support and never replaced by zero.

Uncertainty comes from a paired participant-cluster bootstrap: 2,000 draws
with a fixed analysis seed resample participants with replacement, keep
every selected participant's complete windows, recompute each arm's
confusion counts, and average the paired differences over the five model
seeds and three calibration draws inside each bootstrap draw. Percentile
95\% intervals are conditional on the fitted models and calibration sets.
Overlapping windows are never resampled individually. If more than 5\% of
draws have an undefined endpoint, the interval is omitted.

Two input-grid controls separate mechanisms. The float-QDQ control feeds
each INT8 artifact's quantize--dequantize image of the inputs --- using
that artifact's own scale and zero point --- through the float model, which
measures the input-grid intervention alone. The clip-only control clips
inputs to the artifact's representable range without re-gridding, which
separates range truncation from input resolution. Clipping is counted with
the actual quantization operation (round half-to-even, then saturate), per
input slot and per window, with true-class breakdowns on the evaluation
components only. These comparisons describe the measured error components;
they are not an additive causal decomposition of every error.

File size is the exact serialized artifact size. The device observations
below predate this study and describe the shipped artifacts only; none of
the 110 new artifacts has been device-timed. Device latency is measured
inside the application's \texttt{classify} method with
\texttt{System.nanoTime}; it includes buffer writes, input quantization when
needed, model execution, and output reading/dequantization. The timer stops
before softmax, class selection, and construction of the returned prediction;
model loading and UI updates are also outside its scope. Measurements were
recorded during replay on a Samsung Galaxy S22 Ultra (SM-S908E, Android 16).
The float statistics use the latest 100 inferences, while the recorded INT8
statistics contain 21 inferences after a model switch. The application reports
p95 as the sorted sample at zero-based index
$\lfloor0.95(n-1)\rfloor$, so these estimates also have different rank
resolution. The timings cover this portion of the inference path during replay.
They measure neither the full classification call nor the kernel alone, and
the runs were not controlled for a benchmark comparison.

The iOS client times the same region with the monotonic
\texttt{DispatchTime} clock. Measurements were recorded on an iPhone 18 Pro
Max (iPhone19,3, iOS 27.0.1) during 16$\times$ replay of the synthetic trace,
which produces one inference every 312.5 ms. Each model's statistics use its
latest 100 inferences, and the measurement was repeated in two rounds. The application code was a debug build; LiteRT itself is a prebuilt
release binary. A separate test-runner benchmark calls each model 100 times
back to back after one warm-up call, in three runs. It isolates the timed
region from the interval between replay ticks.
Calibration preparation time, peak memory, model-load time, and energy were
not measured.

Cross-runtime parity was tested with 20 synthetic golden windows, four selected
from each float-predicted class and including values above the INT8 input range.
The test also verifies the SHA-256 hash of each bundled artifact. Float logits
must match the Python output within $10^{-5}$; dequantized INT8 outputs are
required to match bit-for-bit because both sides dequantize identical integer
outputs with the same float32 operation. The iOS suite applies the same
vectors and tolerances and also checks the class-name order. It runs on the
physical iPhone, on the iOS simulator, and in continuous integration.

\subsection{Calibration effects on directional recall}
\label{sec:maineffects}

Table~\ref{tab:calibmain} and Figure~\ref{fig:recall} give the main result

\subsection{Mechanism: clipping, not resolution}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} decomposes the sequential-200 loss with the
input-grid controls. Feeding the float model the clip-only image of the
inputs reproduces essentially the entire 13.35-point float-to-INT8
difference (float$-$clip $13.14$; float$-$QDQ $13.12$), so the coarser
input grid itself costs nothing measurable and the remaining
INT8-internal difference is 0.2 points. Under the sequential-200 range, 27.8\% of held-out windows
have at least one clipped input slot by the rounded-code rule ---
60.9\% of \texttt{falling\_fast} windows, the class that collapses ---
against none under the randomized range: 3.5\% of held-out windows
exceed its real-valued top of 400.8~\mgdl\ at the 401 sensor cap, but
those readings round into the top code, so no input slot clips. Wider range coverage therefore did not trade recall
for resolution in this model: the grid-resolution cost of covering
39--401~\mgdl\ was unmeasurable.

\subsection{Historical comparison}

The earlier revision reported accuracy 0.5184 and macro recall 0.5060 for
float and 0.5866/0.4135 for INT8. Those numbers came from a different
denominator --- the uncorrected aggregate frame, with context-target

\subsection{Deployment costs, and historical device observations}

Calibration coverage has no deployment cost in this model. All 100 matrix

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What nominal sample count hides}
\label{sec:calibevidence}

Official guidance asks for a small representative calibration set
\cite{googleRepresentativeData,googleLiteRTQuantization}, and prior PTQ work
treats clipping, range estimation, and rounding as the levers
\cite{banner2019ptq,nagel2020adaround,hubara2021smallcalibration}. This
study's sharpest observation is that \emph{nominal sample count said
nothing}: the sequential-200 and uniform-200 sets have identical size and
differ by 13.4 points of held-out directional recall, while the 200- and
1,000-window uniform sets differ by nothing. What mattered was measured
coverage --- participants spanned and, through them, the input range.
Overlapping sliding windows make this failure easy to produce: the
toolchain-default pattern of taking the first $N$ windows concentrates an
entire calibration set inside a few hours of one or two sensors.

Once the selection is randomized at all, the refinements evaluated here
are exhausted. Stratifying by input-range cells on top of participant
balancing --- the prespecified primary contrast --- moves the endpoint by
six hundredths of a point. The histogram and min--max observers agree.
The feared resolution trade-off, where covering 39--401~\mgdl\ coarsens
the grid by 60\%, costs nothing measurable, consistent with a label that
depends on the difference between readings rather than their absolute
code. For this model class, calibration design reduces to one rule:
do not let contiguous window order pick your calibration data.

The mechanism controls localize the sequential-arm error at the input
edge: clipping alone reproduces the loss, the quantized grid without
clipping is harmless, and the INT8 interior contributes a quarter point.
The class asymmetry follows directly --- 61\% of \texttt{falling\_fast}
windows contain a reading above the sequential range, since fast falls
start high. A model that cannot see how high the trajectory started
cannot recognize a fast fall.

\subsection{Scope of the accuracy illusion}

The sequential-arm INT8 model is a worse clinical-shape classifier with
better accuracy (0.570 vs.\ 0.507): clipping pushes predictions toward
\texttt{stable}, the class holding three quarters of the weight.
Any pipeline that gates a quantized model on raw accuracy would have
promoted the broken artifact. Directional (minority-class) recall, or any
class-balanced metric, exposes it immediately
\cite{brodersen2010balanced}. This is the practical reading of the study
for small imbalanced time-series deployments.

Parity and runtime agreement are necessary but orthogonal checks: the
Python, Kotlin, and Swift clients agree bit-exactly on dequantized INT8
outputs for the tested vectors, yet parity says nothing about whether the
artifact's input range fits the population. Runtime agreement also does
not validate the labels, cohort split, probability calibration, clinical
safety, or usefulness to a person with diabetes.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The results should be read with the following limitations in mind.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item This is a locked, corrected reanalysis, not a fresh test. The
    held-out participants were scored once before under the flawed
    aggregate protocol, and the five-class decision was informed by a
    historical test run. The protocol, samplers, and endpoint were frozen
    before the new held-out labels were read, but prior exposure cannot be
    undone by re-splitting \cite{kapoor2023leakage}.
    \item One dataset, one cohort, one architecture, one quantizer. The
    source cohort comprised older adults with long-standing type-1
    diabetes and blinded CGM collection \cite{weinstock2016risk}; no
    cross-dataset validation was performed
    \cite{ghimire2024generalize}, and the null results for range
    stratification, budget, and observer are statements about this
    2,909-parameter CNN with per-tensor affine input quantization, not
    about PTQ in general. Larger models, other datasets, or other
    quantizers may behave differently
    \cite{bouthillier2021variance,hubara2021smallcalibration}.
    \item Stride-one windows remain overlapping and dependent
    \cite{bergmeir2018crossvalidation}; uncertainty therefore comes from
    participant-cluster bootstrap intervals conditional on the fitted
    models and calibration sets, with model-seed and draw variation
    averaged inside each bootstrap draw rather than treated as
    independent participants.
    \item The gap-fill mask removes windows around missing readings, but
    the retained grid still contains formatter re-gridding between
    adjacent readings, and interpolation precedes windowing, so
    retrospective preprocessing may use information unavailable in real
    time. The protocol froze three sensitivity tiers (exact-observed
    windows; gap-filled inputs with gap-free endpoints; any gap-filled
    endpoint); none of the three has been evaluated, so results for
    windows outside the primary mask are unreported, not implied.
    \item The five labels are deterministic transformations of a future
    CGM point, not expert annotations, and the CGM-only model omits meal,
    insulin, activity, illness, and other covariates. Probability
    calibration and event-level performance were not evaluated.
    \item None of the 110 new artifacts has been device-timed. The phone
    observations in Table~\ref{tab:deployment} are historical, from two
    devices differing in hardware, OS, LiteRT version, and timer, with
    unequal Android sample counts and uncontrolled conditions. Energy and
    memory were not measured.
    \item The application replays synthetic or locally extracted
    historical traces. It does not ingest a live sensor stream and has
    not undergone prospective, external, usability, or clinical
    validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reproducibility and Ethics}

\subsection{Code, artifacts, and computational record}

The repository contains the training, conversion, benchmark, Android, iOS,
and test code. The bundled artifacts were generated from repository commit
\texttt{361148a} with seed 0. Their SHA-256 digests are
\texttt{eb96c7e...08e6} (float) and \texttt{0dc35387...ea29} (INT8); full hashes
are stored beside the assets and asserted by instrumentation tests. Python unit
tests cover label boundaries, segment-aware windows, model shape, conversion
helpers, synthetic replay behavior, and the provenance audit's split
bookkeeping. Android unit tests cover replay,
buffering, quantization, latency aggregation, and view-model behavior. A merged-
manifest check enforces the absence of Internet permission. The iOS test
suite covers the same units and the golden-vector parity. Continuous
integration runs it on a simulator, together with the source scan for
networking APIs.

The conversion requirements record LiteRT Torch 0.9.1, TorchAO 0.17.0, and
Python LiteRT 2.1.5. They also document PyTorch 2.12.1 and NumPy 2.5.0 in the
historical environment, although the top-level requirements pinned older

\section{Conclusion}

On a corrected, provenance-masked protocol with five training seeds and
100 paired INT8 artifacts, calibration-data coverage fully determined
whether static INT8 quantization damaged this compact CGM trend
classifier. The toolchain-default sequential selection, whose 200 windows
span two participants and cap the input range at 251~\mgdl, cost 13.4
points of held-out directional recall while raising raw accuracy --- an
illusion a class-balanced metric exposes. Any randomized selection of the
same 200 windows restored float-level directional recall; range-aware
stratification, a five-fold budget, and a min--max observer moved it by
at most eight hundredths of a point; and clip-only controls attributed the loss to input-range
clipping rather than grid resolution. Every INT8 artifact is
byte-identical in size and selection takes milliseconds, so the corrected
calibration is free at deployment time. In this setting, coverage means
covering participants; sample count, stratification, and observer choice
were the wrong knobs.

The conclusions are scoped to one cohort, one architecture, and one
quantizer, on a reanalysis of previously touched data. Device timing of
the new artifacts under a paired-round protocol, the exact-coincidence
sensitivity tier, and confirmation on an unused public cohort remain
future work. \project remains a research prototype, not a medical device.
