\begin{abstract}
\project, an Android/iOS research prototype, classifies 12 five-minute
continuous glucose monitor (CGM) readings into five classes of the mean
rate of change over the next 15 minutes. We ask how
quantization-calibration coverage affects directional (minority-class)
recall, and which changes improve it in staged, pre-registered sweeps.
For a 2,909-parameter convolutional network in 8-bit integers (INT8), range stratification, the primary contrast, was null; random
selections already covered the input range. In a prespecified secondary
contrast, contiguous first-$N$ selection, capping inputs at
250.9~\mgdl, cost 13.4 points of held-out directional recall (95\% CI
10.3--16.9) against uniform random selection; a clip-only control
attributes the loss to clipping; the fix is free to deploy. Two small
cohorts did not confirm frozen predictions; post hoc,
difference-encoded inputs (variant chosen on held-out data) cut the
effect to 0.5 points. Rounds one to three reused one Weinstock test
split. Round one's macro-averaged F1 (macro-F1) winner passed every gate
but was rejected post hoc: held-out INT8 directional recall fell (0.517
to 0.438). Round five's seed-4 artifact (flatten head, cosine schedule,
validation-chosen epoch), now bundled in the research applications,
passed all gates on a never-evaluated 2008 trial cohort by $+0.20$
points [$+0.11$, $+0.29$], against round three's 1.1-point seed spread,
but lowered macro-F1 there (0.410 vs.\ 0.427) and failed two gates on
both Weinstock components. \project is not a medical device.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) sampled every five minutes produces 12
readings per hour, from which a near-future glucose value, event, or trend
can be predicted \cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}. This paper studies such
predictions for research purposes, not to guide treatment.

These targets define different learning problems (value regression, event
prediction, trend classification), and studies further differ in horizon,
contextual inputs, cohort, split construction, and metric. Reviews,
comparison indices, public benchmarks, and cross-dataset experiments
document these differences and help separate within-dataset performance
from transfer \cite{liu2023systematic,facchinetti2011index,
sergazinov2024glucobench,ghimire2024generalize}, so scores from
incompatible protocols are not directly comparable.

Commercial CGM trend arrows are related but not equivalent: they summarize
recent or current direction and rate with device-specific rate bands and
look-back intervals \cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend},
whereas \project labels each window by the mean rate of change over the
next 15 minutes, from the current sample and the sample 15 minutes later
--- a research representation that neither replicates a manufacturer's
algorithm nor gives a dosing rule.

Deployment raises further questions: conversion may change a model's
outputs, the client may need different input handling, and lower precision
does not guarantee lower latency on a given device. Class imbalance can
hide conversion errors, because accuracy can rise as predictions
concentrate on a majority class while minority-class recall declines
\cite{brodersen2010balanced}.

This paper asks two research questions. The first is how
quantization-calibration coverage affects the minority-class recall and
deployment cost of a compact glucose trend classifier. Static 8-bit integer
(INT8) post-training quantization (PTQ) sets activation ranges from a set
of calibration inputs \cite{jacob2018quantization,pytorchPT2E2026}, and
coverage means the input values and trajectories that set represents.
Unqualified, calibration here means this quantization step, neither CGM
sensor calibration against blood glucose nor calibration of predicted
probabilities. The second question is which model and training-data
changes improve directional recall, the mean recall (sensitivity) of the
four non-stable classes, when model selection runs in staged rounds that
may each spend at most one pre-registered confirmatory read of protected
labels.

Representative calibration data is established guidance: converter
documentation asks for a few hundred samples ``randomly chosen, in no
particular order'' \cite{googleRepresentativeData}. This work measures,
with participant-clustered intervals and a clip-only control, how
contiguous first-$N$ (dataset-order) selection of overlapping, time-ordered
windows violates that guidance, and the class-level consequence: the
clipped inputs concentrate in the fast-falling class, whose recall
collapses while accuracy rises (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism}
and~\ref{sec:calibevidence}). The question arose in this project's own
pipeline, whose conversion script calibrated the originally bundled INT8
artifact on the first 200 validation windows: 17.5 hours of one
participant's readings, whose maximum lies below readings found in a
quarter of training windows (Section~\ref{sec:provenance}). Earlier
revisions scored the same held-out participants under an uncorrected
protocol (Appendix~\ref{app:history}), so the study is a locked, corrected
reanalysis rather than a fresh test.

The calibration study holds the architecture, loss, quantizer, and data
split fixed and varies only calibration selection: four strategies
(sequential, the contiguous first-$N$ selection; uniform random;
participant-balanced random; participant-balanced range coverage), two
nested budgets (200 and 1,000 windows), three random draws, and five
training seeds, giving 100 INT8 artifacts evaluated against their five
float sources, plus a ten-artifact min--max-observer arm. Its hypotheses,
samplers, budgets, endpoint, bootstrap, and missing-class rules were frozen
before any held-out label was read (Table~\ref{tab:protocolmap},
Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}).

The second question is addressed by five rounds of staged greedy selection
(Section~\ref{sec:sweepprotocol}) that train on Weinstock or, from round
two on, on cohorts of the public DiaData collection \cite{cinar2026diadata}
(Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}, Appendix~\ref{app:cohorts}), select on Weinstock validation windows, and
decide each candidate with frozen gates. Rounds one to three confirmed on
the Weinstock test components that the calibration study had already read,
and round five on RT-CGM, a randomized-trial cohort that no round had
evaluated (Table~\ref{tab:readledger}, Appendix~\ref{app:cohorts}). This
paper reports:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a corrected, provenance-masked evaluation protocol for
    GlucoBench-formatted CGM windows, with eligible-window and class-support
    audits published alongside the code;
    \item a controlled answer to the coverage question
    (Section~\ref{sec:maineffects}): in a prespecified secondary contrast,
    contiguous first-$N$ selection lost 13.4 points of held-out directional
    recall (95\% confidence interval (CI) 10.3--16.9) against uniform
    random selection of the same size, which restored float-level recall.
    The prespecified primary contrast, range coverage versus
    participant-balanced random, was null ($+0.06$ points, CI
    $-0.02$ to $+0.13$) and could not probe range coverage, because both arms
    received the same input range. Where every randomized selection covers
    the full input range, as in this pool, stratification, a five-fold
    budget, and the observer choice move recall by at most about a tenth of
    a point;
    \item mechanism controls that attribute the loss to input-range
    clipping rather than input-grid resolution or internal quantization:
    the clip-only input image, fed to the float model, reproduces 13.14
    points of the sequential arm's 13.35-point float-to-INT8 loss
    (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism});
    \item deployment-cost accounting: every INT8 artifact of the
    2,909-parameter study model has the same size and operator inventory,
    selection takes under 0.03~s, and a prespecified nine-artifact iPhone
    benchmark found the calibration arms within 0.05~$\mu$s of each other,
    so the corrected calibration is free at deployment. INT8 showed no
    mean-latency advantage over float in any
    measurement and saves 31\% of file size for the study model and 70\% for
    the bundled one;
    \item two exploratory follow-ups: on two small further cohorts the
    frozen predictions were not confirmed (Dubosson $+2.0$ points, exact
    sign-flip $p=0.094$; Hall inconclusive, its premise failed;
    Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}), and in a post-hoc experiment
    difference-encoded inputs shrank the uniform-versus-sequential
    difference to $+0.5$ points (Table~\ref{tab:delta},
    Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}); and
    \item five staged accuracy sweeps (Sections~\ref{sec:sweepresults}
    and~\ref{sec:sweepdiscussion}); four spent one pre-registered
    confirmatory read each and round four, whose candidate converted to the
    champion's own artifacts, none: a macro-averaged F1 (macro-F1) winner
    that passed every gate but was rejected post hoc because held-out INT8
    directional recall fell from 0.517 to 0.438; DiaData-only training,
    bundled after round two; a label-definition stability check; and the
    seed-4 flatten-head artifact (cosine schedule
    \cite{loshchilov2017sgdr}, validation-chosen epoch), now bundled in the
    research applications, which passed all gates on never-evaluated RT-CGM
    by $+0.20$ points (CI $+0.11$ to $+0.29$), a fifth of round three's
    1.1-point float seed spread on Weinstock held-out (its own RT-CGM seed
    spread was not measured), but failed two gates on both Weinstock
    components and lowered RT-CGM macro-F1 (0.410 against 0.427); PEDAP is
    a near-tie (0.539 against 0.541), and its four changed factors are not
    separately identified.
\end{itemize}

Source code, tests, synthetic replay data, and the bundled model artifacts
(released for non-commercial research use only) are in the project
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/mohamedmenasy/GlucoEdge}; release
  v1.1.0 is archived on Zenodo, \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23426171}.}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Forecasting tasks, horizons, and inputs}

Forecasting from CGM has expanded from
autoregressive value prediction on sensor history
\cite{reifman2007predictive} to recurrent and attention models with
contextual inputs \cite{mirshekarian2019lstm}, recurrent variance estimation
\cite{martinsson2020variance}, and convolutional--recurrent fusion of CGM,
insulin, and meals \cite{li2020crnn}. Architecture and input modality change
together across these studies, so their results do not isolate model family;
cross-dataset evaluation asks instead whether a trained model survives a change
of dataset, cohort, and collection process \cite{ghimire2024generalize}.
Table~\ref{tab:relatedwork} summarizes these design differences, separating
offline predictive evaluation from reported device execution.

\subsection{Benchmarks and evaluation protocols}

The split determines what a study can claim about generalization:
subject-dependent splits evaluate people represented during training,
held-out-participant splits evaluate unseen people in the same dataset, and
cross-dataset evaluation also changes the cohort and pipeline. GlucoBench
standardizes preprocessing, splits, and baselines across five public datasets
\cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}; experiments across four datasets directly test
changes in data source \cite{ghimire2024generalize}. The calibration study
uses Weinstock, GlucoBench's largest type-1 diabetes cohort
\cite{weinstock2016risk}; the accuracy sweeps also train on five DiaData
cohorts \cite{cinar2026diadata} and evaluate zero-shot, with models that
never trained on them, on PEDAP (Pediatric Artificial Pancreas trial)
\cite{wadwa2023pedap} and RT-CGM, the Juvenile Diabetes Research
Foundation (JDRF) CGM randomized trial \cite{jdrf2008cgm}
(Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}).

Nearby and overlapping windows are dependent, so time-series splitting requires
care: cross-validation validity for autoregressive prediction depends on the
residual structure \cite{bergmeir2018crossvalidation}, leakage can answer a less
demanding question than the one claimed \cite{kapoor2023leakage}, and even with
a fixed split, training and implementation choices contribute benchmark
variance \cite{bouthillier2021variance}. Results therefore need to state
their participant, temporal, and dataset boundaries.

\subsection{Trend representations and class imbalance}

Most Weinstock labels are stable and faster changes are rare, so overall
accuracy weights the stable class by its prevalence. Macro-averaged recall
(balanced accuracy in this single-label setting) weights each class equally
and makes minority-class degradation visible \cite{brodersen2010balanced},
though it does not establish event-level or clinical performance.

No published baseline exists for this exact task (five rate-of-change
classes at a future sample, scored on held-out participants). The
nearest classification study predicts three glucose \emph{level} bands
(hypo-, eu- and hyperglycemia) for 11 participants whose data were
recorded every 15 minutes, so its 15-minute horizon is one step ahead;
its best models reach recalls of 0.85--0.98, with euglycemia recall of
0.59--0.70 at 1~h, under within-patient splits
\cite{kistkins2023classification}. Level bands inherit the level's
autocorrelation, three classes are coarser than five rate bands, and
within-patient splits exploit person-specific dynamics that a held-out
protocol denies.

Rate and direction are already graded in this domain. Continuous
glucose-error grid analysis (CG-EGA) pairs a point-accuracy grid with a
rate-error grid \cite{kovatchev2004cgega} and has been applied to glucose
predictors \cite{zanderigo2007prediction}; the J index combines a
forecast's regularity with its time gain \cite{facchinetti2011index}; and
coherence penalties discourage predicted changes that contradict the
measured ones \cite{debois2019coherent}. Because the target here is a
class rather than a value, this paper reports per-class recall
(sensitivity) and its directional mean, which expose the minority-class
losses that accuracy hides. Per-class recall counts every misclassification
alike: it does not distinguish an adjacent rate band from a reversed
direction, weight errors by glucose level, or grade timing, all of which
CG-EGA and the J index address.

\subsection{Edge inference and quantization}
\label{sec:ptqrelated}

One-dimensional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) extract local temporal
features with compact feed-forward networks \cite{kiranyaz2021survey}. Mobile
inference systems can reduce execution cost through compression and
runtime-specific scheduling \cite{lane2016deepx}, but latency is a property of
model, runtime, and hardware together, so smartphone and tiny-device
benchmarks measure specified workloads on specified platforms
\cite{ignatov2019aibenchmark,banbury2021mlperftiny}. Glucose predictors have
been run on such hardware: Li et al. timed a convolutional--recurrent
forecaster on Android \cite{li2020crnn}, and Zhu et al. embedded a recurrent
predictor in a wearable whose low-power system-on-chip performs real-time
glucose prediction and hypoglycemia detection \cite{zhu2023iomt}. Both
forecast values, not trend classes (Table~\ref{tab:relatedwork}).

Integer quantization approximates floating-point tensors with discrete values
parameterized by a scale and zero point \cite{jacob2018quantization}; static
PTQ estimates activation ranges from calibration inputs before conversion
\cite{pytorchPT2E2026,googleRepresentativeData}.
Low-bit studies show that clipping, range estimation, and rounding choices
can determine the error \cite{banner2019ptq,nagel2020adaround}, and
that accurate PTQ is attainable from small calibration sets
\cite{hubara2021smallcalibration}.

Which samples calibrate the quantizer is itself a studied lever. Standard
INT8 recipes set activation ranges from a small calibration
set \cite{krishnamoorthi2018quantizing,nagel2021whitepaper}, and range
estimators (maximum, entropy, percentile) have been compared across vision,
speech, and language networks \cite{wu2020integer}. In language models, the
choice of calibration data alone produces substantial variation in
downstream performance after quantization or pruning
\cite{williams2024calibration}; in vision, PTQ accuracy tracks how closely
the calibration set's feature statistics match the source data's, more than
whether it comes from the same domain \cite{yu2021crossdomain}; and
data-free methods synthesize calibration inputs that match a network's
batch-normalization statistics \cite{cai2020zeroq}. Converter documentation
states the practical rule: the representative dataset is ``a small subset
of a few hundred samples randomly chosen, in no particular order, from the
training or evaluation dataset'' \cite{googleRepresentativeData}, and the
LiteRT (formerly TensorFlow Lite) guide asks for around 100--500 training or
validation samples \cite{googleLiteRTQuantization}. That calibration data
must represent the deployment inputs is therefore established guidance, not
a finding of this paper.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $g_t$ be the CGM sensor reading at time $t$, in \mgdl, with observations
every five minutes. The input is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t = [g_{t-55}, g_{t-50}, \ldots, g_t] \in \mathbb{R}^{12}.
\end{equation}
The target is the mean rate of change over the next 15 minutes, computed
from a point outside the input window:
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \frac{g_{t+15}-g_t}{15}\quad [\mgdl/\mathrm{min}],
\end{equation}
not the instantaneous rate at $t+15$. The class $y_t$ is assigned by
\begin{equation}
y_t =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{falling\_fast}, & r_t \leq -2,\\
\texttt{falling},       & -2 < r_t \leq -1,\\
\texttt{stable},        & -1 < r_t < 1,\\
\texttt{rising},        & 1 \leq r_t < 2,\\
\texttt{rising\_fast}, & r_t \geq 2.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The 12 readings span 55 elapsed minutes (``one-hour history'' means 12
samples at a five-minute cadence); the history length and 15-minute horizon
are fixed engineering choices for this prototype. The five-class target, a
simplification based on two samples, does not describe the full intervening
trajectory. The boundaries are trend-arrow rate bands ($\pm1$ and $\pm2$~\mgdl/min),
the rates that CGM trend arrows commonly separate; the five classes
collapse device-specific arrow taxonomies
\cite{pettus2017recommendations,rodacki2021trend}. No threshold was
optimized on the test labels.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset and preprocessing}

The experiments use the Weinstock data distributed through GlucoBench. The
source study was a case-control study of severe hypoglycemia in adults aged
60 or older with type-1 diabetes for at least 20 years: 101 cases had at
least one severe hypoglycemic event in the prior year, 100 age-matched
controls had none in the prior three years, and cases showed greater
glucose variability \cite{weinstock2016risk}. The cohort is therefore
enriched for hypoglycemia and variability relative to older adults with
type-1 diabetes in general, which can shift the class prevalences below
and limits how far the results transfer. CGM data were collected blinded,
so participants could not react to their readings: the trajectories lack
the corrections that a real-time display or closed-loop control would
prompt, and deployed use may see different dynamics. GlucoBench lists the
sensor as a Dexcom G4 and reports 200 participants before preprocessing and
192 afterwards, at a five-minute sampling interval
\cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}; an audit of the formatter output
reproduces both counts. Scaling is disabled, so glucose stays in raw
\mgdl. The formatter rounds timestamps to the minute, splits each
participant's record at gaps longer than 45 minutes, drops segments with
fewer than 240 original readings, linearly interpolates each remaining
segment onto a five-minute grid that starts at its first reading, and tags
it with an \texttt{id\_segment}. Of the 579,782 grid values, 75.8\%
coincide with an original reading, 19.8\% fall between two consecutive
readings no more than 7.5 minutes apart (reading times drift relative to
the grid), and 4.4\% fill one or more missing readings. Because
interpolation precedes windowing, interpolated values may occur among the
inputs or at target samples. The configured split holds out
10\% of participants (20 people) as an out-of-distribution
(held-out-participant) component and
forms temporal validation and test components from the rest; the formatter
combines the test components into one frame, which
Section~\ref{sec:eligible} separates again by row ownership. All results
report the held-out-participant and temporal components separately.

The readings span exactly 39--401~\mgdl. Dexcom G4 systems report 40 to
400~\mgdl\ and display LOW or HIGH outside that range, and the data pile up
at the two values just beyond it: among the distinct readings in eligible
held-out windows (Section~\ref{sec:eligible}), 922 equal 39~\mgdl\ and
1,253 equal 401~\mgdl, against 33 at 40 and 22 at 400. The two values are
therefore consistent with stored LOW and HIGH codes (the source data
dictionary was not checked). A code bounds the true value rather than
measuring it, so a window whose current or endpoint value is coded has a
label rate that is itself only a bound. Of the eligible held-out windows,
6.6\% contain a coded input and 4.2\% a coded endpoint (temporal: 4.7\%
and 2.9\%); by true class, the share with a coded input runs from 3.3\%
(\texttt{falling}) to 9.8\% (\texttt{falling\_fast}), and coded endpoints
concentrate in \texttt{stable} (5.4\%, against 0.7--1.7\% in the
directional classes). Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}
(Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}) includes a check that excludes them.

Windows are generated independently within each $(\texttt{id},
\texttt{id\_segment})$ group, in time order, and emitted only when all 12
input points and the three following horizon steps fit in the same
segment, so a sensor gap or participant boundary is never treated as a
continuous trajectory. The stride is one reading, matching an application
that updates every five minutes; adjacent windows therefore overlap heavily
and are statistically dependent. The frames yielded 424,462 training,
55,289 validation, and 127,165 aggregate test windows from 172, 127, and
186 participants: 57,068 test windows from the 20 held-out participants and
70,097 from temporal segments of 166 training participants.

Table~\ref{tab:distribution} shows the resulting class imbalance; both fast
classes exceed 13,000 training windows. A preliminary 20-epoch run obtained
test recalls of 0.5333 (\texttt{falling\_fast}) and 0.5380
(\texttt{rising\_fast}), so neither met the predeclared condition for
collapsing the classes (fewer than 200 training examples or recall below
0.30), and the classifier keeps all five. Test recall informed this
five-versus-three decision, an exploratory development choice rather than
independent validation.

\subsection{Split roles, observation mask, and eligible windows}
\label{sec:eligible}

GlucoBench repeats rows from a preceding split at the top of validation and
in-distribution test frames so sequence models can form initial histories.
Earlier revisions let every prefix row start a window, so 13.6\% of
aggregate test windows scored targets in validation-owned rows and 35.1\%
of validation windows duplicated training windows
(Table~\ref{tab:provenance}, Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}). The corrected protocol assigns every
formatter row to the earliest split that lists it and scores a window only
when its anchor (last input sample) and all three forecast rows are owned
by that split; earlier-split readings may serve as causal input history,
and scored $(\text{participant}, \text{segment},
\text{target time})$ keys are asserted disjoint across splits.

Because the formatter interpolates before windowing, a second mask removes
gap filling: a grid value is retained only if it lies on or between two
original readings at most 7.5 minutes apart. An audit showed that requiring
exact coincidence with an original reading would discard roughly 40\% of
windows for grid drift unrelated to missing data, so the gap-fill rule is
the primary mask and exact coincidence a sensitivity tier. A window is
eligible when its 12 inputs and future endpoint are all gap-free; the two
intermediate forecast steps are not label inputs and are not masked.
Table~\ref{tab:eligible} shows the result: eligible training windows retain
all five classes with at least 10,330 examples (\texttt{falling\_fast}),
and the calibration pool keeps 127 of 172 development participants.

\subsection{Model and training}

The calibration study uses \texttt{TrendCNN}, a one-dimensional CNN on
tensors of shape $(B,1,12)$: a convolution from 1 to 24 channels, a second
from 24 to 36 channels (both with kernel size 3, stride 1, and padding 1),
a rectified linear unit (ReLU) after each, adaptive average pooling over
time, and a 36-to-5 linear head, 2,909 trainable parameters in all. Later
experiments mostly keep this family (round three also tried a temporal
convolutional network \cite{bai2018tcn}) and vary its input encoding, width, pooling head,
loss, and training data; the models bundled in the applications over
the project's history range from 2,909 to 50,645 parameters and were
trained on Weinstock or on DiaData cohorts \cite{cinar2026diadata}.
Table~\ref{tab:variants} (Appendix~\ref{app:cohorts}) lists every bundled
variant with its architecture,
encoding, loss, training data, quantization-calibration pool, and artifact
hash; Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the shared workflow.

Training uses inverse-frequency class-weighted cross-entropy, batch size
64, and Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 20 epochs
\cite{kingma2015adam,paszke2019pytorch} in the calibration study and its
follow-up experiments (Section~\ref{sec:sweepprotocol} gives the sweeps'
settings). The study trains five checkpoints, seeds 0--4, on the eligible
windows of Section~\ref{sec:eligible}; the fixed split does not change with
the training seed. Validation loss was observed after every epoch, with no
early stopping or test-time selection. Metrics were computed with
scikit-learn and the study's own confusion-count code
\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. Because the test distribution is dominated by
\texttt{stable}, the primary summary is the unweighted mean recall
(per-class sensitivity) of the four directional classes (directional
recall); five-class macro recall, equivalent to balanced accuracy, is also
reported \cite{brodersen2010balanced}.

Two input-dependent baselines use the same eligible windows: a recent-slope
baseline that maps $(g_t - g_{t-15})/15$, from the inputs alone, through
the same five thresholds, and a multinomial logistic regression on the 12
readings with training-only standardization, balanced class weights, and
fixed $C=1$ (converged). A constant-\texttt{stable} classifier shows the
effect of prevalence.

\section{Conversion and Deployment}

\subsection{Conversion and quantizer}

Every checkpoint is converted with LiteRT Torch, the PyTorch converter for
LiteRT (Google's on-device inference runtime, formerly TensorFlow Lite):
float conversion with a fixed $(1,1,12)$ input signature, and static INT8
PTQ through PyTorch 2 Export (PT2E) with LiteRT Torch's
\texttt{get\_symmetric\_quantization\_config} helper
(\texttt{is\_per\_channel=True}, \texttt{is\_dynamic=False}). Despite the
helper's name, only the weights are quantized symmetrically, per channel;
activations, the model input included, use per-tensor affine quantization
whose ranges a histogram observer selects to minimize quantization error
\cite{pytorchPT2E2026}. The observer always includes zero, so with zero
point $-128$ an input range runs from 0 to 255 times the input scale, and
the codes below the lowest CGM reading go unused. The project's conversion
script originally took its 200 calibration windows from the start of the
validation set in dataset order, with no randomization or range balancing
(Section~\ref{sec:provenance}). In the converted graph only the final
fully connected layer executes in int8; both convolutions run in float32
on dequantized tensors (Section~\ref{sec:deploycost}).

\subsection{Calibration experiment design}
\label{sec:design}

The study freezes the checkpoint, quantizer, and conversion pipeline and
varies only which windows of the calibration pool, the 27,109 eligible
validation windows, calibrate the histogram observer; no sampler reads
future labels, and selection code is tested to be invariant under label
shuffling. Four strategies are compared. \emph{Sequential} is contiguous
first-$N$ (dataset-order) selection: the first $N$ windows in deterministic
(participant, segment, time) order, as the project's original conversion
script took them; the guidance this pipeline cites asks for random
selection (Section~\ref{sec:calibevidence}). \emph{Uniform random} is a seeded
permutation of the pool. \emph{Participant-balanced random} cycles a
per-draw shuffled participant order round-robin, taking one window from a
fixed per-participant permutation each visit and skipping exhausted
participants. \emph{Participant-balanced range coverage} reuses exactly
that participant allocation but, within each participant, cycles through
low/middle/high range cells defined by the pool's own 10th and 90th
percentiles of per-window input minimum and maximum, so it differs from
balanced random only in within-participant input-range coverage.

Each strategy is drawn at budgets of 200 and 1,000 windows, the 200-window
set nested as the first 200 of the 1,000-window ordering. Stochastic
strategies use three seeded draws (101--103); every selection is shared
across the five model seeds, giving 100 INT8 artifacts (5 seeds $\times$
[2 sequential + 3 strategies $\times$ 2 budgets $\times$ 3 draws]).
A mechanism-control arm re-quantizes two cells (sequential-200 and
range-coverage-200, draw 101) with a min--max activation observer in place
of the histogram observer, adding 10 artifacts. For every calibration set
the study records participants, segments, unique input readings, input
extremes and quantiles, and occupied range cells, because a nominal window
count can cover few distinct readings; for every artifact it records input
quantization parameters, size, and SHA-256.

\subsection{Client implementations}

For input scale $s$ and zero point $z$, both applications use the affine form
$q = \operatorname{clip}(\operatorname{round}(x/s)+z, -128,127)$,
$\hat{x}=s(q-z)$, with quantization parameters read from the model
metadata rather than hardcoded. All three implementations divide in single
precision and round exact half-way values to the even integer --- Python
with \texttt{numpy.round}, Kotlin with \texttt{kotlin.math.round}, Swift
with \texttt{rounded(.toNearestOrEven)}
\cite{numpyRound,kotlinRound,swiftRounding} --- so they produce identical
integer inputs for any glucose value.

The Kotlin/Jetpack Compose application and its SwiftUI iOS mirror replay a
bundled synthetic trace through a 12-reading ring buffer that resets after
a cadence gap, mirroring training-time segment boundaries, and classify
every complete window locally with the float artifact by default or INT8
for comparison. Both run byte-identical models through LiteRT's
\texttt{CompiledModel} API \cite{googleLiteRTCompiledModel} (LiteRT 2.1.0
on Android, the 2.3.0 release binaries on iOS); on real devices both use
its CPU accelerator, which executes the graph through the XNNPACK
delegate, with no further options; neither sets a thread count. The
merged Android manifest is tested to ensure it does not request the
\texttt{INTERNET} permission; iOS has no equivalent, so a source scan
fails the continuous-integration build if networking APIs appear in the
application code, a weaker guard. Both applications also offer an
optional on-device caption of the replayed window (``Explain''; Apple
Foundation Models on iOS, LiteRT-LM on Android), instructed never to give
advice, recommendations or dosing and labelled ``On-device demo note ---
not medical guidance''; the captions were not evaluated.
Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails} describes the iOS packaging and an
emulator workaround.

\section{Experimental Protocol}

\subsection{Calibration study protocol}
\label{sec:calibprotocol}

Evaluation uses two disjoint components from Section~\ref{sec:eligible},
never concatenated: the 46,376 eligible windows of the 20 held-out
participants (primary) and the 37,361 eligible temporal windows of 166
training participants (secondary). The protocol (samplers, budgets,
endpoint, bootstrap, and missing-class rules) was frozen and committed
before any held-out label was read, in \texttt{paper/results/}
\texttt{calibration-coverage/protocol.json}; its hypotheses H1--H4 are
stated in the study plan that file names, committed at the freeze commit
it records (\texttt{d63cc8f}). Table~\ref{tab:protocolmap}
(Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}) maps each hypothesis and contrast to its
outcome. A provenance audit (\texttt{experiments/split\_audit.py}) maps
every window to the formatter rows it uses (Section~\ref{sec:eligible}),
reading row identities and timestamps for all splits but glucose values
only from training and validation rows; it also records the originally
bundled calibration set and that artifact's input quantization parameters.

With class support $n_c$, $N=\sum_c n_c$, and class recall $R_c$, accuracy
is $A=\sum_{c=1}^{5}(n_c/N)R_c$ and macro recall
$R_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{c=1}^{5}R_c$; the primary endpoint is
the unweighted mean recall of the four directional classes. The
prespecified primary contrast is range-coverage versus participant-balanced
random at budget 200, which was designed to isolate within-participant
input-range coverage from participant representation. Secondary contrasts are range
coverage versus uniform random, 1,000 versus 200 within each strategy, the
budget-by-strategy interaction, and uniform random versus sequential at
each budget, the last added by a protocol amendment before any held-out
label was read; the study plan marks secondary intervals descriptive.
\texttt{protocol.json} names the interaction without a formula, so
Section~\ref{sec:maineffects} reports the difference between two
strategies' 1,000-versus-200 effects, a definition fixed after the primary
analysis was published. A recall is undefined on zero support and never
set to zero.

Uncertainty comes from a paired participant-cluster bootstrap: 2,000 draws
with a fixed analysis seed resample participants with replacement, keep
every selected participant's complete windows, recompute each arm's
confusion counts, and average the paired differences over the five model
seeds and three calibration draws inside each bootstrap draw. Percentile
95\% CIs are conditional on the fitted models and calibration sets;
model-seed and calibration-draw variation are reported separately.
Overlapping windows are never resampled individually. Draws with an
undefined endpoint are dropped before the percentile step, and if more
than 5\% of draws are undefined, the interval is omitted.

Two input-grid controls separate mechanisms. The float quantize--dequantize
(QDQ) control feeds the float model each INT8 artifact's QDQ image of the
inputs, with that artifact's own scale and zero point, which measures the
input-grid intervention alone; the clip-only control clips inputs to the
artifact's representable range without re-gridding, which separates range
truncation from input resolution. Clipping is counted with the actual
quantization operation (round half-to-even, then saturate), per input slot
and per window, with true-class breakdowns on the evaluation components
only. These comparisons describe the measured error components, not an
additive causal decomposition of every error.

Two follow-up experiments reuse this machinery under their own frozen
protocols. A second-cohort run repeats a reduced matrix on two further
GlucoBench cohorts, Dubosson \cite{dubosson2018d1namo} and Hall
\cite{hall2018glucotypes}: five seeds, sequential versus uniform random at
budget 200, and one directional prediction per cohort, frozen before that
cohort's evaluation labels were read (Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}). A
delta-encoding run, a post-hoc experiment on the already-read Weinstock
held-out data, retrains the model on re-encoded inputs under a frozen float
gate, robustness prediction, and ship gate (Table~\ref{tab:delta}).

File size is the exact serialized artifact size. A prespecified iPhone
benchmark times nine artifacts, the seed-0 float artifact and 8 of the 110
study INT8 artifacts; the other 102 study INT8 artifacts and all
second-cohort and delta-encoded artifacts were not timed. Cross-runtime
parity tests run 20 synthetic golden windows through Python, Kotlin, and
Swift and verify each bundled artifact's SHA-256 hash.
Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails} gives both protocols.

\subsection{Accuracy sweep protocol}
\label{sec:sweepprotocol}

The second research question asks which model and training-data changes
raise the directional recall of the bundled INT8 model. Five rounds of
staged greedy selection addressed it (Section~\ref{sec:sweepresults}):
each stage trains a few arms that differ in one factor (two in round
one's width-by-window grid and round five's recipe arm), compares their
seed means, and carries the winner forward, and a final stage picks one
seed. Selection reads only the 27,109 eligible Weinstock validation
windows of Section~\ref{sec:eligible}, whatever the training data, with
three seeds per arm in rounds one and two (five at their final seed
stage) and five from round three on.
Arms within 0.1 point of each other are tied and the cheaper arm wins
(fewer parameters first); Appendix~\ref{app:sweepdetails} records how
rounds three and five applied this rule.

Round one selected on validation macro-F1 and confirmed on held-out
macro-F1. Its outcome (Section~\ref{sec:sweep1}) changed the endpoint:
from round two on, selection and confirmation use directional recall,
guarded by a stable-recall floor that disqualifies an arm at selection if
its mean validation stable recall falls more than 5 points (rounds two to
four) or 2 points (round five) below the champion's. The champion is the
pair bundled when a round began: the delta-encoded pair
(Table~\ref{tab:delta}) in rounds one and two and the round-two winner in
rounds three to five (Table~\ref{tab:variants}).

From round two on, three gates decide a candidate: (1) its INT8 model
beats the champion's INT8 model on the endpoint, with the 95\% interval's
lower bound above $-0.5$ points; (2) its INT8 stable recall is at most 2
points below the champion's; (3) its INT8 directional recall is within
0.5 points of its own float. Round one applied gate~1 to macro-F1, used
the float-proximity rule as its second gate, and reserved its third for
ensembles. A round spends at most one confirmatory read: its protocol
file (\texttt{protocol\_sweep\_r$N$.json}) is committed before any
protected label is read, and the evaluation code refuses to run until it
is. Rounds one to three read the Weinstock held-out (primary) and temporal
(secondary) components, and from round two PEDAP as a descriptive probe.
Round five moved its primary endpoint to RT-CGM, a cohort no round had
evaluated, and declared the Weinstock components and PEDAP non-gating
secondaries. A candidate whose converted artifacts are SHA-256-identical
to the champion's spends no read (round four's outcome, made a rule in
round five), so four rounds spent one read each and round four none;
Table~\ref{tab:readledger} lists every read of held-out, temporal and
RT-CGM labels in this work. No protocol stated when a gate-passing
candidate may be overridden. The two cases that arose fit one rule:
override only if the candidate loses directional recall on the primary
component (round one), not for losses on components the protocol declared
non-gating (round five).

Round one trained on the eligible Weinstock training windows, its pooling
stage adding the Dubosson and Hall training windows. Rounds two to five
drew on DiaData v3 \cite{cinar2026diadata}, using only its raw
(non-imputed) five-minute archive. An overlap and sensor audit
(\path{diadata_audit_r1.json}) admitted six type~1 diabetes (T1D) cohorts
with 1,001 subjects, excluding DiaData's copies of the Weinstock and
Dubosson studies and cohorts with a non-five-minute or unrecorded sensor
interval, RT-CGM among them. Of the six, five (CITY, DCLP3, REPLACE-BG,
SENCE and WISDM; 898 subjects) fed training, and PEDAP (103 subjects)
trained no model.
Mixtures were capped near 15 million training windows per arm by seeded
whole-subject sampling, so no subject is split. Round three reopened
RT-CGM, DiaData's label for the JDRF CGM randomized trial, for one
training arm, and round five used it as its primary endpoint;
Table~\ref{tab:cohorts} characterizes every cohort.

All sweep models take the delta-anchor input of Table~\ref{tab:delta} (11
consecutive differences and the last reading divided by 16) over a
12-reading window, except arms that change the input. Training uses Adam
with learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 20 epochs, keeps the final epoch, and
uses the study's inverse-frequency class-weighted cross-entropy (CE)
unless a stage varies the loss. Round one trained with batch size 64 on
the CPU, as the calibration study did; rounds two to five trained with
batch size 512 on an Apple-silicon graphics processor through Metal
Performance Shaders (MPS), chosen by a smoke run
(Appendix~\ref{app:sweepdetails}). Round five's recipe arm decays the
learning rate to zero on a cosine schedule over the 20 epochs
\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr} and keeps the epoch with the highest validation
directional recall among epochs meeting the stable floor.

Each candidate was converted as in Section~\ref{sec:design} with two of
the study's calibration selections, rebuilt on delta-encoded inputs from
the study's pool (the 27,109 eligible Weinstock validation windows) and
index lists: sequential-200 (diagnostic) and uniform-200, draw 101 (the
candidate artifact). The validation split thus served both selection and
INT8 calibration, and because the round-two winner and every later
candidate trained on no Weinstock window, their INT8 calibration is
cross-cohort. Confirmatory contrasts pair both models' predictions on the
same windows, keyed by participant and anchor time, and use the paired
participant-cluster bootstrap described above (2,000 draws, a seed frozen
per round). Each
contrast compares one candidate artifact, the best validation seed, with
one champion artifact: the interval resamples participants, is
conditional on both fitted models, and carries no training-seed
variation.

\section{Results: Calibration Coverage}

\subsection{Split and calibration provenance}
\label{sec:provenance}

Table~\ref{tab:provenance} (Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}) summarizes
the audit. No test window has a target
in a training row, and none repeats a training window. The context-prefix
overlap instead affects 17,266 test windows (13.6\%) whose targets fall in rows
that GlucoBench assigns to validation; validation labels were not used for
fitting or checkpoint selection. Most validation frames begin with 192
training rows of model context, so 19,402 validation windows (35.1\%) repeat
training windows exactly.

The originally bundled artifact's calibration set is narrower than its size
suggests: its 200 overlapping windows come from one participant and one
segment, 211 distinct readings spanning 17.5 hours, and 178 of the 200
windows repeat training windows. Its converted input scale, 0.9408~\mgdl\
per code with zero point $-128$, gives an input range of 0--239.9~\mgdl,
the quantized image of the largest calibration input, 240.0~\mgdl;
Figure~\ref{fig:range} (Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}) shows how much of
the development data lies above that ceiling. These historical facts
motivated the controlled study.

\subsection{Coverage manipulation}

The sampling interventions changed measured coverage, not just the
strategy label (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). The sequential-200 set draws
from 2 of 127 pool participants and 224 unique readings with a maximum of

\subsection{Calibration effects on directional recall}
\label{sec:maineffects}

Table~\ref{tab:calibmain} and Figure~\ref{fig:recall} give the main result
on the held-out-participant component. Float LiteRT is prediction-identical
to its PyTorch checkpoint at every seed. With the sequential-200
calibration, mean directional recall drops from 0.487 to 0.354 while
accuracy \emph{rises} from 0.507 to 0.570, the accuracy illusion the
originally bundled artifact showed, reproduced under the corrected
protocol; with any randomized 200-window selection, INT8 matches float to
the third decimal (0.487). \texttt{falling\_fast} is the most
sensitive class: 0.550 float, 0.236 under sequential-200, 0.547 under
uniform-200.

Table~\ref{tab:contrasts} reports the prespecified contrasts. The primary
contrast, range coverage versus participant-balanced random at 200, is
$+0.06$ percentage points (95\% CI $-0.02$ to $+0.13$), but it could not
test H1 (that broader input-range coverage beats random selection with the
same participant allocation) as posed: both arms reach the pool's 39--401~\mgdl\ span and
receive the same 0--400.8~\mgdl\ input range (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}),
so they quantize every input identically and differ only in the windows
that set the internal activation ranges. Uniform random versus sequential
at 200, a prespecified secondary, is $+13.38$ points (CI $+10.29$ to
$+16.88$). The effect appears at every training seed (13.05 to 14.14
points, standard deviation (SD) 0.47 across seeds), while the float
model's own directional recall has an across-seed SD of 1.34 points and
the three uniform draws within a seed an SD of 0.02 to 0.21 points. Within
this shared-range regime, raising a randomized strategy's budget from 200
to 1,000 changes recall by at most $+0.08$ points (only the
balanced-random interval, $[+0.02, +0.15]$, excludes zero), and the
budget-by-strategy interactions among the three randomized strategies lie
between $-0.05$ and $+0.06$ points (temporal: up to $+0.07$), every
interval including zero.
Sequential is the exception: its 1,000-window set reaches the full range
and gains $+13.35$ points over its 200-window set, so its interaction with
uniform random is $-13.33$. Given the same windows, the min--max observer
moves seed-mean recall by at most 0.12 points (0.354 vs.\ 0.354
sequential; 0.488 vs.\ 0.487 range coverage). The temporal component
mirrors every pattern (Table~\ref{tab:contrasts}).

\subsection{Mechanism: clipping, not resolution}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} decomposes the sequential-200 loss with the
input-grid controls. The clip-only image of the inputs, fed to the float
model, reproduces essentially the entire 13.35-point float-to-INT8
difference (float$-$clip $13.136$); re-gridding the clipped inputs moves it
by only 0.01 points (float$-$QDQ $13.125$), and the INT8 interior adds 0.23
points. Under the sequential-200 range, 27.8\% of held-out windows have at
least one clipped input slot by the rounded-code rule (60.9\% of

under the randomized range: the 3.5\% of held-out windows that contain the
401~\mgdl\ code exceed its real-valued top of 400.8~\mgdl, but that value
rounds into the top code, so no input slot clips. Under uniform-200, the
coarser input grid alone changes float recall by $+0.20$ points
(float$-$QDQ $-0.20$; no interval computed), so covering
39--401~\mgdl\ cost no measurable recall through resolution in this
model.

On held-out participants the five-seed float CNN reaches 0.487 directional

\subsection{Two further cohorts}

Dubosson (nine participants with type-1 diabetes, readings to 400~\mgdl)
and Hall (57 mostly non-diabetic participants, maximum 303~\mgdl) each
received the reduced matrix described with the protocol. Each cohort's
protocol froze one prediction, without a decision rule or margin:
Dubosson's uniform$-$sequential difference would be positive; Hall's
would be near zero, because with almost no readings above 240~\mgdl\ a
sequential selection could not meaningfully under-cover the range.
Dubosson's held-out component is a single participant, so its protocol
designates the temporal component (six participants) and reports held-out
descriptively; Hall designates held-out (six participants) and keeps
temporal (50) secondary. Both designated contrasts' frozen percentile
intervals include zero, and with six clusters cluster-based inference
tends to over-reject \cite{cameron2008bootstrap}, so
Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}
replaces them with an exact sign-flip test over
participants: each participant's additive contribution to the pooled
difference (mean over seeds and draws; the contributions sum to the
difference) has its sign flipped in all $2^6=64$ patterns, so the smallest
attainable two-sided $p$ is 0.031.

Each prediction's outcome is reported separately. For Dubosson, the
designated difference is positive, $+1.98$ points, but one participant
contributes $+1.62$ of it (the other five $-0.04$ to $+0.16$) and the
sign-flip test gives $p=0.094$: the point estimate has the predicted sign,
and the data do not separate it from zero. For Hall, the designated
held-out difference, $+1.31$ points ($p=0.25$; one participant contributes
$+1.08$, the other five $-0.09$ to $+0.21$), is inconclusive: without a
frozen margin it supports neither a positive effect nor a near-zero one. The premise behind the Hall
prediction failed: it reasoned from the share of readings above
240~\mgdl, the historical ceiling, but the sequential-200 set reached only
165.0~\mgdl. Hall's 50-participant temporal component shows $+4.31$
$[+1.14, +7.34]$ points.

The following reading is exploratory. The mechanism predicts the effect
from the share of windows a sequential ceiling clips beyond those uniform
calibration clips, not from the distance in \mgdl\ between that ceiling
and the cohort maximum. Weinstock's sequential-200 ceiling of
250.9~\mgdl\ clips 27.8\% of held-out windows against none under uniform
calibration, and costs 13.4 points. In the small cohorts the sequential
selection's clipped share exceeds uniform calibration's by at most 4.3
percentage points (not at all for Dubosson's held-out participant), and
the effects are 0.3 to 4.3 points. Among the three components with excess
clipping the shares do not order the effects (Dubosson temporal has the
largest excess, 4.3 percentage points, but a smaller effect than Hall
temporal, excess 3.1), so no finer dose-response is claimed. Unlike
Weinstock, uniform calibration itself under-covers both small cohorts: its
ceilings are 352.7--354.5~\mgdl\ against Dubosson's 400 maximum and
189.9--233.9~\mgdl\ against Hall's 303, and it still clips 10.9\% of
Dubosson temporal windows. In every cohort the sequential selection again
drew on two early participants, and uniform calibration sits within about
a point of the float model. Per-seed records:
\path{confirmation_dubosson.json}, \path{confirmation_hall.json};
contributions, sign-flip tests, and clipped shares:
\path{panel_revision_r1.json}.

\subsection{Delta-encoded inputs}
\label{sec:deltainputs}

If calibration failures are input-range failures, re-encoding the input
should remove them. This post-hoc experiment on previously touched
held-out data had gates frozen beforehand. Two variants were trained from
scratch on the same eligible windows and seeds: 11 consecutive
differences, and the same differences plus the last absolute value divided
by 16, placing a coarse level channel inside the delta range. Both passed
the frozen float gate (five-seed held-out directional recall
$\geq 0.482$): differences alone reach 0.497 and differences plus the
scaled anchor 0.509, against the absolute-input model's 0.487. The frozen
rule advanced the higher-scoring variant, so the anchor variant was chosen
on held-out data (0.5088 against 0.4969), and its margin over the absolute
model is selection-affected.

Because activations, the input included, are quantized per tensor, the
anchor model's differences and level channel share one input step, set by
the calibration windows: 0.247 per code under contiguous first-$N$
selection (0.247~\mgdl\ for differences, $16\times$ that for the level,
which saturates at 320~\mgdl) and 0.231, 0.713 and 1.054 under the three
uniform-200 draws (level ceilings 511, 833 and 776~\mgdl), against the
absolute model's 1.572~\mgdl\ under uniform selection
(\path{panel_revision_r1.json}). Under contiguous first-$N$
selection, which costs the absolute-input model 13.4 points, the delta
model loses half a point against its own float ($+0.50$
$[+0.33, +0.67]$), and uniform minus sequential is $+0.54$
$[+0.38, +0.72]$ (Table~\ref{tab:delta}, Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}).
The frozen prediction of a
near-zero difference again had no margin; the measured difference is a
twenty-fifth of the absolute model's but excludes zero, so the encoding
makes INT8 far less sensitive to calibration selection, not insensitive.
The frozen ship criterion (sequential-INT8 within 0.5 points of float)
passes at 0.4955 points, a margin of less than five thousandths of a
point, while the interval above straddles the threshold. The delta pair
then bundled used the uniform selection, under which the five-seed float
and INT8 means agree to the third decimal (Table~\ref{tab:delta}; bundling
history in Appendix~\ref{app:history}).
Full record: \texttt{delta\_inputs\_r1.json}.

\subsection{Deployment costs}
\label{sec:deploycost}

Calibration coverage has no deployment cost in this model. All 110 study
INT8 artifacts serialize to exactly 11,976 bytes regardless of strategy,
budget or observer (float: 17,352 bytes, a 31.0\% reduction) and share a
single operator inventory, so no change in operators or size is
attributable to any calibration arm; the 40 second-cohort and 20
delta-encoded INT8 artifacts have the same size and operator inventory
(\texttt{artifacts} in \texttt{panel\_revision\_r1.json}). The bundled
50,645-parameter pair serializes to 208,560 (float) and 61,936 bytes
(INT8), a 70.3\% reduction (\texttt{MODELS.md}). Offline
preparation is also negligible: every sampler selects its windows in under
0.03~s, and INT8 export averages 0.5~s for a 200-window set and 0.8~s for
a 1,000-window set on the study host.

Inside the artifact, calibration changes more than the input pair. Between
the sequential-200 and uniform-200 artifacts of one checkpoint, 12 of 41
graph tensors differ: the graph input, the three activation tensors
re-quantized between operators, the output, and the int32 bias of the
final layer, whose scale follows its input's, plus six layout-operator
copies of those parameters; the three int8 weight tensors are identical.
The artifact is also less integer than its name: both convolutions, their
bias additions, and the average pool run in float32 on dequantized
tensors, and only the final fully connected layer runs in int8
(per-operator types in the same file). The prespecified iPhone benchmark
(Table~\ref{tab:devicebench}, Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}) matches
this picture: the eight calibration arms differ by at most 0.05~$\mu$s in
mean latency (3.42--3.47~$\mu$s), so the calibration fix carries no
latency cost on this phone, and INT8 is slower than float in every arm
($+0.64$ to $+0.70~\mu$s), as expected of a graph that adds quantize and
dequantize steps to the same float convolutions.

\subsection{Round one: a macro-F1 winner rejected after its read}
\label{sec:sweep1}

Round one asked whether loss design, capacity, window length, pooling
across cohorts or seed ensembling improves the delta-encoded classifier
(\path{protocol_sweep_r1.json}). Stage~A compared four losses at the
study architecture (validation macro-F1, three-seed means). A
distance-penalized CE, which adds the expected class distance
$\lambda \sum_j p_j\,|j-y|$ with $\lambda=0.5$ to the weighted CE (a
variant of class-distance-weighted CE \cite{polat2022cdwce}),
scored 0.427, ahead of weighted CE (0.381), ordinal label smoothing
(soft ordinal labels \cite{diaz2019softlabels}, here moving 0.15 of the
target mass to the adjacent classes; 0.390) and focal loss with $\gamma=2$
\cite{lin2017focal} (0.380). Stage~B crossed channel widths with window
length under that loss: (96,144) channels (42,725 parameters) reached
0.439, and a 2-hour window gained at most 0.04 points at any width.
Pooling the Weinstock, Dubosson and Hall training windows (Stage~C) lost
1.6 points, and a five-seed mean-logit ensemble (Stage~D) did not beat
the best single seed (0.4426 against 0.4430), so seed~1 of the (96,144)
distance-penalized model became the candidate.

The read passed every gate (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): the candidate's INT8
model beat the champion's by $+5.68$ macro-F1 points held-out and
$+5.52$ temporally, within 0.1 point of its own float, and all five
candidate seeds beat the champion (five-seed mean $+5.25$ points). The
candidate was nevertheless not bundled: directional recall, the quantity
the bundled models had been selected on, fell from 0.517 to 0.438 held-out and from 0.497
to 0.406 temporally. That drop is the basis of the rejection, a decision
taken after the read (Table~\ref{tab:readledger}) that set the endpoint of
every later round. A per-class read made after the gates were scored
(Appendix~\ref{app:sweepdetails}, Table~\ref{tab:sweeprecall}) shows an
operating-point shift toward \texttt{stable}: F1 rose in every class, half
of the summed gain from \texttt{stable}, while recall fell in three of the
four directional classes. Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r1.json}.

\subsection{Round two: DiaData training under a directional endpoint}

Round two returned to the weighted CE and asked whether more real data,
and how it is mixed, raises directional recall
(\path{protocol_sweep_r2.json}). Stage~M compared five mixtures at
(96,144) channels (three-seed validation means): Weinstock only 0.512;
Weinstock plus the five DiaData training cohorts in natural proportions
0.527 or with equal windows per cohort 0.532; the five DiaData cohorts
alone 0.535; and natural proportions with fast-class oversampling 0.526,
level with natural proportions, as expected once class weights are
recomputed on the oversampled labels. The DiaData-only mixture won with
stable recall 0.540, above the floor; its model never sees a Weinstock
training window. Narrower (24,36) and wider (192,288) channels lost (0.526
and 0.524), and seed~1 led the five at 0.539 (champion INT8: 0.519).

The read passed all three gates (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): $+0.60$ points
held-out, stable recall 0.459 against 0.475, and INT8 within 0.11 points
of float, with the temporal component agreeing in sign. The interval
includes zero, so gate~1 here certifies non-inferiority within 0.5 points,
not superiority. The effect did not hinge on the chosen seed: the five
candidate seeds averaged $+0.55$ points above the champion artifact on
held-out, none below it. Macro-F1 slipped held-out, and on PEDAP (7.0M
windows, on which no model trained) directional recall rose. The
candidate was bundled
after golden parity passed in the Android unit tests on the Java virtual
machine (JVM), on the iOS simulator and on the iPhone~18 Pro~Max (51
tests, INT8 bit-exact). Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r2.json}.

\subsection{Round three: RT-CGM, time of day and a temporal convolutional
network}

Round three staged three changes against the round-two recipe, five
seeds per arm (\path{protocol_sweep_r3.json}). The data stage reopened
RT-CGM, DiaData's copy of the JDRF CGM randomized trial
\cite{jdrf2008cgm}, whose primary report randomized 322 intensively
treated children and adults with T1D in three age groups (8--14, 15--24
and $\geq$25 years); the cohort name denotes this trial, not current
real-time CGM. Appendix~\ref{app:cohorts} gives its sensors, the cadence
re-audit that admitted it, and the 355 of its 451 subjects with scored
windows.

Adding RT-CGM to training lost 0.6 points of validation directional
recall (five-seed means), and sine and cosine time-of-day input channels
lost 0.3 (timestamps taken as device-local time). The architecture stage
replaced the CNN with a causal dilated temporal convolutional network
(TCN) \cite{bai2018tcn}: four residual blocks, kernel 3, dilations 1--8,
72 channels and 63,005 parameters. It edged the CNN (0.533 vs.\ 0.532),
and seed~2 led at 0.537.

The TCN's INT8 model beat the champion's by $+0.77$ points held-out, the
first fully positive interval on the directional endpoint, agreed
temporally ($+0.97$) and sat 0.05 points above its float, but failed
gate~2: stable recall 0.414 against 0.459 held-out ($-4.4$ points against
gate~2's 2-point limit; $-3.8$ temporally). Macro-F1 also fell, and on PEDAP
directional recall rose slightly while stable recall fell
(Table~\ref{tab:sweep}). The candidate was rejected and the round-two pair
stayed bundled. Two selection details shaped round five: seed~2 was picked
by strict argmax although seed~1 trailed by 0.01 points of directional
recall but had higher stable recall (0.536 against seed~2's 0.526), and
the 5-point selection floor admitted a seed that a 2-point floor would already have excluded on validation. The
$+0.77$ points also belonged to the chosen seed: across the five candidate
seeds the mean held-out effect was $+0.05$ points, three of five seeds
were ahead, and their float held-out directional recall spanned 1.1
points. Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r3.json}.

\subsection{Round four: a label-definition stability check and an
identity outcome}

Round four took its arms from the champion's error structure, read
descriptively from round three's saved held-out predictions
(Table~\ref{tab:readledger}): 72\% of champion errors were adjacent-class,
and slow-class errors went to the same-direction fast class (47\% of
\texttt{falling} errors, 46\% of \texttt{rising}) more often than to
\texttt{stable} (25\% each). That read chose arms only; selection stayed
on validation.

Before any arm trained, the round compared two labels on the 27,109
validation windows (\path{exp0_sweep_r4.json}). The endpoint label
classifies $(g_{t+15}-g_t)/15$; an alternative label classifies the
least-squares slope over the four readings $g_t,\ldots,g_{t+15}$; both
use the trend-arrow rate bands ($\pm1$ and $\pm2$~\mgdl/min). The slope
label recovers the endpoint label's directional classes with mean recall
0.954 (\texttt{stable} 0.995), and recomputing the endpoint label after
adding independent Gaussian noise of $\sigma=1$ or 2~\mgdl\ to the
readings gives 0.915 and 0.860. This is a label-definition stability
check. Both labels are functions of the same future readings, so their
agreement bounds nothing that a model seeing only the 12 past readings
can reach, and the recall attainable from CGM-only inputs remains
unknown. White noise of this size is also gentler than published CGM
error models, which add blood-to-interstitial lag, sensor-calibration
drift and autoregressive noise \cite{facchinetti2014sensor}, and the labels are
defined on the sensor trace, which itself lags blood glucose. The abort
rule, committed in the round's design specification before training,
would have stopped the round had the slope label's agreement exceeded the
champion's INT8 validation directional recall (0.533) by less than 2
points;
with both labels sharing the future readings it could hardly fire, and
it did not.

Three stages then varied the objective, the training targets and inputs,
and weight averaging against the round-two recipe at five seeds per arm
(Appendix~\ref{app:sweepdetails}, Table~\ref{tab:sweepfour}); every arm
lost to the weighted-CE control on validation directional recall.
Square-root class weights lowered it to
0.405 while stable recall rose to 0.827, so the full inverse-frequency
weights, with the slow-to-fast over-prediction they cause, are what buys
directional recall here. A Huber regression head on the rate, decoded at
the same rate bands, collapsed toward \texttt{stable} (0.200 directional,
0.950 stable), the pull toward the dense region of the target that
regression on imbalanced targets is known to suffer
\cite{yang2021imbalanced}. A head for conditional ordinal regression
(CORN) \cite{shi2023corn} reached 0.470. Slope labels for training
changed little ($-0.2$ points), and $\sigma=2$~\mgdl\ input noise traded
directional for stable recall ($-3.7$ points). A uniform average of the
five seeds' weights collapsed (0.238 directional, 0.003 stable), the
expected outcome: weight soups average models fine-tuned from one
pre-trained start \cite{wortsman2022soups}, and independently initialized
runs need not share a linearly connected basin \cite{frankle2020linear}.

The surviving candidate, the control arm's best seed, is the champion's
own checkpoint and converts to SHA-256-identical artifacts
(\texttt{96eef43c\ldots}/\texttt{a887ea4e\ldots}). A contrast would have
compared the model with itself, so no confirmatory read was spent and,
beyond the arm-choice read above, no held-out, temporal or PEDAP label was
read; the round-two pair stayed bundled. Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r4.json}.

\subsection{Round five: the seed-4 flatten-head artifact}
\label{sec:sweep5}

Round five tested how the final convolutional features reach the
classifier. The global average pool can erase where in the hour a
movement happened: two synthetic histories carrying the same 10~\mgdl\
rise, placed 30 versus 20 minutes before the last reading, produce
bit-identical outputs from the champion's checkpoint and from both of its
bundled artifacts (\path{recency_collision.json},
Section~\ref{sec:repro}), an existence proof rather than a rate on real
data. A recipe stage compared the round-two recipe with the cosine
schedule and validation-chosen kept epoch of
Section~\ref{sec:sweepprotocol}; a head stage then replaced the pool,
under the winning recipe, with a flatten head in which the $144\times12$
feature map feeds a 1,728-to-5 linear layer (50,645 parameters against
42,725); a final stage picked the seed (\path{protocol_sweep_r5.json}).
Selection took the cosine recipe (five-seed validation means 0.535
against 0.532), then the flatten head (0.538 against 0.535), and seed~4
led at 0.541 directional and 0.546 stable recall, the only flatten-head
seed above the champion checkpoint's own validation float (0.539). These
numbers are selection-biased: the kept epoch and the seed were chosen on
the split they are quoted from. The candidate differs from the champion in head,
learning-rate schedule, kept epoch and seed at once, and its INT8
artifact is calibrated with uniform-200 from the Weinstock validation
pool, a cohort it never trained on.

The read moved to RT-CGM, whose labels no round had evaluated: 12.67M
eligible windows from the 355 of 451 admitted subjects with scored
windows. Round three had trained a rejected arm on this cohort, but
neither model under comparison trained on it, so the comparison is
zero-shot for both. All three gates passed (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): the
candidate's INT8 model beat the champion's by $+0.20$ directional-recall
points [$+0.11$, $+0.29$], a fifth of the 1.1-point float spread of round
three's five seeds on Weinstock held-out, held stable recall within 0.2
points (0.605 against 0.607) and sat 0.25 points under its own float.
That gap includes any cost of input clipping: both artifacts share one
INT8 input range, which 2.8\% of RT-CGM windows exceed (the level channel
saturates at 511~\mgdl). RT-CGM readings span 14--654~\mgdl\
(Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}); DiaData does not record the source of values
outside the 40--400~\mgdl\ span the Dexcom cohorts' codes bound, and
without the 1.2\% of windows holding one the gain is $+0.29$ points
[$+0.21$, $+0.38$] (\path{panel_revision_r1.json}).
The candidate replaced the round-two pair under the rule fixed before the
read, but the result is a choice between two artifacts, not evidence for
a design: round three's chosen-seed effect ($+0.77$) shrank to $+0.05$ at
the five-seed mean, and the five-seed RT-CGM comparison of the two
recipes, which would measure seed variation here, was not run. The four
changed factors are not separately identified. The
non-gating secondaries carry costs: on both Weinstock components the
candidate fails gates~1 and~2 (held-out $-0.32$ points with stable
recall 0.436 against 0.459; temporal $+0.33$ with 0.499 against 0.520),
macro-F1 is lower on all three paired splits, including RT-CGM (0.410
against 0.427), and PEDAP directional recall is 0.539 against 0.541.
PEDAP's per-class changes show where the predictions moved:
\texttt{falling\_fast} recall rose 6.7 points and \texttt{rising\_fast}
2.3, while \texttt{falling} fell 6.5, \texttt{rising} 3.1 and
\texttt{stable} 0.3; the candidate labels 16.0\% of PEDAP windows
\texttt{falling\_fast} against the champion's 12.3\%, and its
\texttt{falling\_fast} precision drops from 0.284 to 0.242, consistent
with an operating-point shift from the slow to the fast classes rather
than better discrimination. Full record: \path{accuracy_sweep_r5.json};
PEDAP per-class detail: \path{panel_revision_r1.json}.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Input-range coverage, not sample count}
\label{sec:calibevidence}

That calibration data matter in PTQ is known
\cite{nagel2021whitepaper,cai2020zeroq,williams2024calibration,yu2021crossdomain};
the converter guidance quoted in Section~\ref{sec:ptqrelated} asks for
samples ``randomly chosen, in no particular order''
\cite{googleRepresentativeData}, and the sequential arm contravenes it by
taking contiguous windows in stored order. What this study adds is a
measurement of that departure where it is easy to make: stride-one CGM
windows overlap, so 200 contiguous windows sit inside about 17 hours of
one or two sensors' readings and their input range stops below the
population's tail. At identical size, the sequential and uniform sets
differ by 13.4 points of held-out directional recall, concentrated in \texttt{falling\_fast}
and placed at the input edge by the clip-only control, while raw accuracy
rises. Sample count did not predict the
loss: the 1,000-window sequential set recovers fully with 6 participants
because it reaches the full range.

The rule this evidence supports is to cover the deployment input range,
which any randomized selection does in this pool: every randomized
200-window set reaches 39--401~\mgdl\ and receives the same
0--400.8~\mgdl\ input range. That shared range also limits what the null
contrasts show. The primary contrast and the randomized budget contrasts
compared arms whose inputs were quantized identically, so they show that,
once the range is covered, stratification, a larger budget, and the
observer choice moved recall by at most about a tenth of a point, not that
range-aware selection is useless. It was not tested where uniform draws
under-cover the range, as in Dubosson and Hall, whose uniform-200 sets
still clip 10.9\% (Dubosson temporal) and 0.5--1.0\% (Hall) of windows
(Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}).
A more direct remedy would remove the input's dependence on calibration
data by fixing its range from the sensor's reporting limits (here
39--401~\mgdl, codes included). The PT2E API offers fixed quantization
parameters (torchao's \texttt{FixedQParamsQuantizationSpec}), but LiteRT
Torch applies them only to sigmoid and softmax outputs, so the input would
need a custom annotation, a route untested here. Unlike difference
encoding, which left a $+0.54$-point dependence
(Section~\ref{sec:deltainputs}), fixed parameters would remove it.

The absent resolution cost is what 8-bit error analysis predicts
\cite{banner2019ptq,nagel2021whitepaper}. Spreading 0--400.8~\mgdl\ over
255 steps gives a step of 1.572~\mgdl, a maximum rounding error of
0.79~\mgdl, and a root-mean-square error of about 0.45~\mgdl\ (the step
over $\sqrt{12}$), small against the 15~\mgdl\ change over 15 minutes that
separates \texttt{stable} from \texttt{falling} or \texttt{rising};
clipping at 250.9~\mgdl\ removes up to 150~\mgdl\ from a reading.

Two accounts of the class asymmetry fit the mechanism controls
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), which localize the sequential-arm error at
the input edge, and the study does not separate them. In one, fast falls
start high --- 61\% of
\texttt{falling\_fast} windows contain a reading above the sequential
range --- and a model that cannot see how high a trajectory started loses
a cue that a steep fall is coming. In the other, clipping maps every
reading above 250.9~\mgdl\ to one code, flattening the clipped part of a
high fast fall into a plateau and erasing its slope as well as its level;
this account would also explain the inflated \texttt{stable} predictions and fits a
label defined by the difference between readings.

\subsection{Scope of the accuracy illusion}

The sequential-arm INT8 model is a worse directional classifier with
better accuracy (0.570 vs.\ 0.507): clipping pushes predictions toward
\texttt{stable}, the class holding three quarters of the weight, so any
pipeline that gates a quantized model on raw accuracy would have promoted
the broken artifact. Directional (minority-class) recall or five-class
macro recall exposes it immediately (0.354 and 0.412 against float 0.487
and 0.494, Table~\ref{tab:calibmain}) \cite{brodersen2010balanced}; this is
the practical reading of the study for small imbalanced time-series
deployments. Not every class-balanced metric guards against a shift toward
\texttt{stable}: round one's macro-F1 rose under one
(Section~\ref{sec:sweep1}).

Parity and runtime agreement are necessary but orthogonal checks: the
three clients agree bit-exactly on dequantized INT8 outputs for the
tested vectors, yet parity says nothing about whether the
artifact's input range fits the population, and validates neither the
labels, the cohort split, probability calibration, clinical safety, nor
usefulness to a person with diabetes.

\subsection{What the sweeps show}
\label{sec:sweepdiscussion}

The sweep program's main lesson is that a pre-registered endpoint can
pass every gate and still optimize the wrong thing. Round one's F1 rose
in every class while directional recall, the quantity the bundled models
had been selected on, fell by 7.9 points; pre-registration fixed how the endpoint was
analyzed, not whether it was the right endpoint. From round two on, the
gates encode that quantity, directional recall under a stable-recall
floor, but the floor is relative to each round's champion. Held-out INT8
stable recall therefore drifted from 0.475 to 0.459 to 0.436 across the
two promotions, the second gated on another cohort; a floor anchored to
a fixed reference would prevent that drift.

What the rounds established is narrower than five rounds of selection
suggest. Round one's gain held at every seed and lay on the wrong axis.
Round two's DiaData-only recipe matched or beat the delta-encoded champion
artifact at every seed on held-out, the most consistent result of the
program, though on a reused split and with a confirmatory interval that
includes zero. Round three showed how much seed choice alone can
contribute ($+0.77$ points for the chosen seed, $+0.05$ at the five-seed
mean), and round four's arms all lost on validation, two in ways prior
work predicts. Round five showed that one artifact passed the
pre-registered gates on a cohort no round had evaluated, by $+0.20$
points against the 1.1-point float spread of round three's five seeds on
Weinstock held-out, while failing gates~1 and~2
on both Weinstock components and lowering macro-F1 on RT-CGM (0.410
against 0.427), with PEDAP a near-tie (0.539 against 0.541). It did not show that a flatten head, a cosine schedule or
validation-chosen epochs improve this task: the four factors changed
together, one seed per side was compared, and the five-seed RT-CGM
comparison was not run. No round measured the recall attainable from 55
minutes of CGM history.

A displayed class is the model's estimate of the rate band that the
sensor-reported glucose will average over the next 15 minutes, neither a
measurement of the current rate nor a reading of blood glucose. On both
evaluated cohorts (Appendix~\ref{app:sweepdetails},
Table~\ref{tab:shippederrors}), 63\% of displayed
movement classes were followed by a mean rate within $\pm1$~\mgdl/min,
while a displayed \texttt{stable} was right in 92\% (held-out) and 94\%
(RT-CGM) of windows; a displayed \texttt{falling\_fast} was followed by a
falling or fast-falling rate in a third (held-out) to a half (RT-CGM) of
windows; and 9--15\% of truly falling or rising windows were shown in the
opposite direction. Every movement class is predicted 1.7 to 5.2 times as
often as it occurs: \texttt{falling\_fast}, the most over-called, pairs
the highest recall with the lowest precision (0.706 at 0.136 held-out,
0.673 at 0.214 on RT-CGM), while \texttt{rising\_fast} has the lowest
RT-CGM recall of any class (0.483); inverse-frequency class weights and a
directional-recall endpoint select this operating point
(Table~\ref{tab:sweepfour}). Below 70~\mgdl\ the model almost never
recognized a fast fall (0 of 1 held-out and 2 of 96 RT-CGM
\texttt{falling\_fast} windows), which alone makes that band's
directional recall the lowest (0.33 held-out, 0.36 RT-CGM); over the
other three directional classes the $\geq$250~\mgdl\ band is lowest
(0.38, 0.44). These figures describe a
research classifier on retrospective data; they are not evidence about
how a person would use its output.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item This is a locked, corrected reanalysis, not a fresh test. The
    held-out participants were scored once before under the flawed
    aggregate protocol, and the five-class decision was informed by a
    historical test run. The protocol, samplers, and endpoint were frozen
    before the new held-out labels were read, but prior exposure cannot be
    undone by re-splitting \cite{kapoor2023leakage}.
    \item The calibration findings rest on one architecture, one
    quantizer, and one primary cohort: a case-control sample of older
    adults with long-standing type-1 diabetes, about half of whom had a
    severe hypoglycemic event in the prior year, recorded with blinded CGM
    \cite{weinstock2016risk}. The range-stratification and budget nulls
    hold in a saturated regime in which every randomized 200-window
    selection receives the same INT8 input range; range-aware selection was
    not tested where random draws under-cover the range. These
    nulls and the observer comparison describe this 2,909-parameter CNN
    with per-tensor affine input quantization, not PTQ in general
    \cite{bouthillier2021variance,hubara2021smallcalibration}.
    \item The second-cohort evidence is exploratory: neither designated
    six-participant contrast is significant at the 5\% level under an
    exact sign-flip test, and Hall's temporal component is positive
    against a frozen near-zero prediction whose premise failed
    (Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}). All other calibration contrasts are
    single-cohort \cite{ghimire2024generalize}.
    \item Stride-one windows remain overlapping and dependent
    \cite{bergmeir2018crossvalidation}, so intervals come from
    participant-cluster bootstraps conditional on the fitted models and
    calibration sets (Section~\ref{sec:calibprotocol}), and
    six-participant components get an exact sign-flip test instead of a
    valid interval.
    \item The accuracy sweeps reuse the main study's Weinstock splits:
    every round selected on its validation windows; rounds one to three
    read its held-out and temporal labels; round four's arm choice, an
    external review's offset scan and this revision's re-analyses reread
    them descriptively; and round five reports them again as non-gating
    secondaries (Table~\ref{tab:readledger}). Round five is an
    artifact-level ship decision between two artifacts
    (Section~\ref{sec:sweep5}): the winner's validation numbers are
    selection-biased, its macro-F1 is lower on all three paired splits,
    and the five-seed RT-CGM comparison that could credit the flatten head
    or the cosine schedule was not run. No round had evaluated RT-CGM
    labels before, though a rejected round-three arm trained on it.
    \item The gap-fill mask removes windows around missing readings, but
    the retained grid still contains formatter re-gridding between
    adjacent readings, and interpolation precedes windowing, so
    retrospective preprocessing may use information unavailable in real
    time. The frozen sensitivity tiers were evaluated after the primary
    analysis was published, and the gap-filled-endpoint tier's labels
    score agreement with the formatter's interpolated grid, not with
    unobserved sensor readings. Readings of 39 and 401~\mgdl, consistent
    with LOW and HIGH codes, are treated as glucose values, so a window
    that starts or ends at one carries only a bound on its rate
    (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} excludes such windows in a check).
    \item The five labels are deterministic transformations of the
    sensor trace, not expert annotations, and that trace lags blood
    glucose through blood-to-interstitial diffusion and carries
    sensor-calibration drift and autocorrelated noise \cite{facchinetti2014sensor}.
    Round four checked only label-definition stability, also under
    independent Gaussian noise of $\sigma=1$ and 2~\mgdl, a gentler
    perturbation than published autoregressive sensor-error models
    \cite{facchinetti2014sensor}; the recall attainable from 12 past CGM
    readings is unknown. The CGM-only model omits meal, insulin, activity,
    illness, and other covariates, and probability calibration and
    event-level performance were not evaluated.
    \item Wear and therapy regimes differ across cohorts. Weinstock
    participants wore blinded CGM; most DiaData cohorts mix masked run-in
    and real-time wear, which DiaData does not separate, and DCLP3 and
    PEDAP include closed-loop insulin delivery (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}).
    The future 15-minute trajectory is therefore not the same
    physiological object across cohorts, and a deployed trend display
    would itself change the wearer's behaviour and so the trajectories it
    predicts. PEDAP randomized preschool children to automated insulin
    delivery or standard care \cite{wadwa2023pedap}. Every cohort behind
    the bundled model has type-1 diabetes; no type-2 diabetes, pregnancy,
    or inpatient data entered its training or evaluation.
    \item Device evidence is narrow. Of the 110 study INT8 artifacts, 8
    (with one float artifact) were timed on one iPhone; the other 102, and
    the 60 INT8 artifacts of the second-cohort and delta-encoding runs, were
    not device-timed, though all 170 share one byte size and one operator
    inventory. Repeated calls on one phone are not evidence across
    devices, the historical Android device is no longer available,
    initialization was measured within-process, and energy and memory were
    not measured (Appendix~\ref{app:calibdetails}). INT8 showed no mean-latency
    advantage over float in any measurement; its size saving is 31\%
    (5,376 bytes) for the 2,909-parameter study model and 70\% (146,624
    bytes) for the 50,645-parameter bundled model. The quantization trade-off studied here
    is an accuracy risk against a size saving, with no measured speed
    benefit.
    \item The application replays synthetic or locally extracted
    historical traces. It does not ingest a live sensor stream and has
    not undergone prospective, external, usability, or clinical
    validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reproducibility and Ethics}
\label{sec:repro}

\subsection{Code, artifacts, and computational record}

The repository contains the training, conversion, benchmark, Android, iOS,
and test code. The bundled artifacts are the round-five seed-4
flatten-head pair (cosine schedule, validation-chosen epoch), whose
confirmatory protocol was frozen at repository commit \texttt{f9010b6}
before the read. Their SHA-256 digests are \texttt{bfb37468...6153}
(float) and \texttt{eacc0a74...f554} (INT8); full hashes are stored beside
the assets and asserted by the golden-parity tests, and the application's
model manifest (\texttt{MODELS.md}) records every bundled pair's hashes

\subsection{Data availability and research-use scope}

The CGM data are public research data. The Weinstock
\cite{weinstock2016risk}, Dubosson \cite{dubosson2018d1namo}, and Hall
\cite{hall2018glucotypes} cohorts are accessed through GlucoBench under the
source datasets' reuse terms \cite{sergazinov2024glucobench}. The sweep
cohorts come from DiaData v3 \cite{cinar2026diadata} under CC BY-NC 4.0,
using only its raw (non-imputed) five-minute archive: CITY
\cite{laffel2020city}, DCLP3 (DiaData label DLCP3) \cite{brown2019closedloop},
REPLACE-BG \cite{aleppo2017replacebg}, SENCE \cite{sence2021cgm}, and WISDM
\cite{pratley2020wisdm} for training; PEDAP \cite{wadwa2023pedap} as a
zero-shot probe; and, as round five's primary endpoint, RT-CGM, the JDRF
CGM randomized trial \cite{jdrf2008cgm}, whose DexCom SEVEN, MiniMed
Paradigm and FreeStyle Navigator sensors (\path{diadata_audit_r1.json})
cannot be assigned to subjects (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}). Some DiaData cohorts originate from the
Barbara Davis Center, Jaeb Center for Health Research, Joslin Diabetes
Center, T1D Exchange, University of Colorado, and University of Virginia;
the analyses, content, and conclusions presented here are solely the
responsibility of the author and have not been reviewed or approved by
these institutions. No CGM data are committed to the \project repository
or the distributed applications, and the committed replay trace is
synthetic. The bundled weights were trained on DiaData cohorts released
under CC BY-NC 4.0, and their INT8 quantization parameters were calibrated
on Weinstock validation windows; the weights are released for
non-commercial research use only. This work makes no diagnosis,
monitoring, treatment, or dosing claim. Its outputs must not be used for
medical decisions. The application displays this restriction in the
primary interface.

The repository's \texttt{MODEL\_CARD.md}, structured after Mitchell et
al.\ \cite{mitchell2019modelcards}, states the bundled model's intended
use (offline research replay), out-of-scope uses (live decision support,
alerts, dosing, and any clinical use), input requirements, evaluation
results, and known failure modes, among them frequent movement labels
during stable glucose and over-calling of the fast classes, together with
the populations its data do not represent.

This secondary analysis used only public, de-identified data under the
providers' terms; no participants were recruited, and no ethics review
was sought.

\section{Conclusion}

On a corrected, provenance-masked protocol with five training seeds and
100 paired INT8 artifacts, the input range covered by the
quantization-calibration windows decided whether static INT8 quantization
damaged this compact CGM trend classifier. Contiguous first-$N$
selection, whose 200 windows span two participants and cap the input
range at 250.9~\mgdl, cost 13.4 points of held-out directional recall in
a prespecified secondary contrast while raising raw accuracy --- an
illusion that directional or macro recall exposes. Any randomized selection
of 200 windows restored float-level directional recall, and clip-only
controls attributed the loss to input-range clipping, not grid
resolution. What mattered was covering the deployment input range, which
any randomized selection did in this pool; once it was covered,
stratification, budget, and observer choice moved recall by at most about
a tenth of a point. These nulls, the null primary contrast among them,
are scoped to that saturated regime: range-aware selection was not tested
where random draws under-cover the range, as they did in the two small
cohorts. The corrected calibration is free at deployment; INT8 brought no
mean-latency advantage in any measurement and saves 31\% of file size for the
2,909-parameter study model and 70\% for the bundled model.

The staged sweeps add a methodological lesson. Four rounds spent one
pre-registered confirmatory read each; round four, whose candidate
converted to the champion's own artifacts, spent none. Gates alone were
not enough: round one's macro-F1 winner passed every gate while
held-out INT8 directional recall fell from 0.517 to 0.438, and its
post-hoc rejection
made a directional endpoint and a stable-recall floor part of every later
protocol. Under those rules, DiaData-only training passed round two's
gates, with an interval that includes zero, and was bundled; no
round-three or round-four arm was adopted. Round four's
label-definition stability check compares two labels computed from the
same future readings, so it bounds nothing that a model of past readings
can reach; the recall attainable from CGM history is unknown. Round five
was a choice between two artifacts: the seed-4 flatten-head artifact
(cosine schedule, validation-chosen epoch), now bundled in the research
applications, passed all gates on never-evaluated RT-CGM by $+0.20$
points, a fifth of round three's 1.1-point float seed spread on Weinstock
held-out (its own RT-CGM seed spread was not measured), but failed
gates~1 and~2 on both Weinstock components and lowered RT-CGM macro-F1
(0.410 against 0.427); pediatric PEDAP directional recall was a near-tie
(0.539 against 0.541). Its head, schedule, kept epoch, and seed changed
together and are not separately identified.

The calibration conclusions cover one primary cohort, architecture and
quantizer, on a reanalysis of previously touched
data; both headline findings hold in every frozen sensitivity tier. On
two small further
cohorts the frozen predictions were not confirmed: Dubosson's designated
difference was $+2.0$ points (exact sign-flip $p=0.094$), and Hall's
$+1.3$ points ($p=0.25$) was inconclusive, its premise failing when the
contiguous selection reached only 165~\mgdl. That evidence is
exploratory. \project is a research prototype, not a medical device.

\subsection{Earlier revisions}

After the July results, neither the September revision, the provenance
audit that followed it, nor the 10 October iOS revision retrained a model,
re-evaluated one, or touched held-out glucose values. September reused the
July results and computed its descriptive summaries from the rounded
tables; the audit reran only the GlucoBench formatter and window
construction; and the iOS revision added the client and its device
observations on unchanged artifacts with the same SHA-256 hashes.

Those revisions reported accuracy 0.5184 and macro recall 0.5060 for
float and 0.5866/0.4135 for INT8. These numbers came from a different
denominator --- the uncorrected aggregate frame, with context-target
overlap and no gap mask --- and from the original sequential calibration,
so they are not comparable to Table~\ref{tab:calibmain} row by row. The
qualitative pattern they showed (accuracy up, every directional recall
down) is what the corrected protocol reproduces for the sequential arm.

\subsection{Historical device observations and parity runs}

Before the study, the originally bundled artifacts were observed on two
phones (Table~\ref{tab:deployment}). On a Samsung Galaxy S22 Ultra
(SM-S908E, Android 16), latency was measured inside the application's
\texttt{classify} method with \texttt{System.nanoTime}: buffer writes,
input quantization when needed, model execution, and output
reading/dequantization, stopping before softmax, class selection, and
construction of the returned prediction (model loading and user-interface
updates excluded). The float statistics use the latest 100 inferences, the
recorded INT8 statistics 21 after a model switch, and p95 is the sorted
sample at zero-based index $\lfloor0.95(n-1)\rfloor$, so the estimates
differ in rank resolution. These timings measure neither the full
classification call nor the kernel alone, and the runs were not controlled
for a benchmark comparison. The iOS client timed the same region with the
monotonic \texttt{DispatchTime} clock on the iPhone 18 Pro Max during
16$\times$ replay of the synthetic trace (one inference every 312.5~ms),
the latest 100 inferences per model in two rounds, in a debug application
build over the prebuilt release LiteRT binary; a separate test-runner
benchmark called each model 100 times back to back after one warm-up call,
in three runs. Neither phone showed an INT8 latency advantage (iPhone
back-to-back means 2.83--2.85~$\mu$s float, 3.69--3.72~$\mu$s INT8), the
same picture as the study's benchmark.

All three Android instrumentation tests passed on the physical device: the
\texttt{CompiledModel} path loaded both artifact hashes, matched float
logits within $10^{-5}$ on all 20 golden windows, and matched INT8
dequantized outputs exactly. These July device tests ran an earlier Kotlin
version that rounded ties toward positive infinity; the golden-vector
inputs, given to 0.1~\mgdl, contain no tie at the original input scale, so
the expected outputs are unchanged. All four iOS parity tests passed on
the iPhone with the same result --- artifact hashes, class-name order,
float logits within $10^{-5}$, and bit-exact INT8 outputs --- using the
half-to-even input rounding the July Android run predates. Python, Kotlin,
and Swift therefore agreed on the tested vectors; model outputs may still
differ on untested inputs, although input quantization now follows the
same rounding rule on both sides.

\section{Cohorts, Model Variants, and Held-Out Read Ledger}
\label{app:cohorts}
Table~\ref{tab:cohorts} characterizes every cohort used in this work,
Table~\ref{tab:variants} the model variants bundled at some point, and
Table~\ref{tab:readledger} every analysis that read held-out, temporal
or RT-CGM labels, in date order.

RT-CGM needs more provenance than the table holds. The DiaData audit lists
DexCom SEVEN, MiniMed Paradigm and FreeStyle Navigator sensors
(\path{diadata_audit_r1.json}), and DiaData records neither the device nor
the wear mode behind a subject's data. A re-audit admitted all 451
subjects (modal within-segment interval 300\,s and $\geq$95\% of
intervals within 270--330\,s; \path{diadata_audit_r2.json}), but that
rule could not fail: DiaData rounds timestamps to a five-minute grid
\cite{cinar2026diadata} and segments split at gaps over 330\,s, so every
within-segment interval is 300\,s by construction. The 96 admitted
subjects without a scored window contain no run of 15 consecutive
five-minute readings; the
other 355 supply the 12.67M windows used in round five. The first audit's
collinear fraction, the share of consecutive same-segment reading triples
whose middle reading is the exact mean of its neighbours, is 0.305 for
RT-CGM against at most 0.229 for the admitted cohorts. The audit does not
split that excess between runs of identical readings, which the statistic
also counts, and linear interpolation. Runs of identical readings on a
five-minute grid could also come from resampling a device with a
different native interval (sample and hold), which would distort the rate
labels of round five's primary cohort.

\subsection{Client packaging, emulator workaround, and parity tests}

The SwiftUI iOS application bundles the same two artifact files and
synthetic trace by reference to the Android asset directory, so both
platforms run byte-identical models, and follows the Kotlin implementation
for replay, the gap-resetting window buffer, the model switch, and the
latency statistics. Real Android devices use LiteRT 2.1.0
\texttt{CompiledModel}; on the tested Apple-Silicon-hosted Android
emulator, native model creation
hit an illegal-instruction fault in a CPU feature probe for the Arm
Scalable Matrix Extension, uncatchable from Kotlin, so emulators are
selected before runtime initialization and use the classic interpreter
with XNNPACK disabled --- a workaround for the tested environment, not a
statement about LiteRT configurations generally.

Cross-runtime parity was tested with 20 synthetic golden windows, four
selected from each float-predicted class and including values above the
INT8 input range. The test also verifies the SHA-256 hash of each bundled
artifact. Float logits must match the Python output within $10^{-5}$;
dequantized INT8 outputs are required to match bit-for-bit because both
sides dequantize identical integer outputs with the same float32
operation. The iOS suite applies the same vectors and tolerances and also
checks the class-name order. It runs on the physical iPhone, on the iOS
simulator, and in continuous integration.

\subsection{Selection, training, and protocol details}

Round three picked its final seed by strict argmax instead of the tie
rule, a disclosed deviation; round five applied the tie rule at every
pick, including the seed and the kept epoch. The champion's validation
references come from its bundled INT8 artifact on the same windows (0.519
directional and 0.563 stable recall for the delta-encoded pair, 0.533 and
0.548 for the round-two pair); stage tables report float checkpoints.
Each round's protocol file records the selection trail, candidate,
champion SHA-256 hashes, prediction, endpoint, gates, bootstrap seed and
pairing rule. For rounds two to five, a pre-sweep smoke run accepted the
largest of batch sizes 512, 256 and 128 whose mean validation directional
recall stayed within 1 point of batch 64 (512 scored 0.513 and 64 scored
0.506, two seeds each), and two same-seed MPS runs reproduced exactly.
The round-five protocol file describes the INT8 calibration pool as
Weinstock training windows; the code and the pool hash show the
validation pool. An external review published between rounds four and
five (\path{paper/analysis/accuracy-review-2026-10-13/}, commit
\texttt{3755d55}) scanned stable-logit offsets on cached round-two and
round-three held-out and temporal predictions (exploratory;
Table~\ref{tab:readledger}); its synthetic recency diagnostic and code
inspection proposed round five's flatten head and its learning-rate
schedule with epoch selection.

\subsection{Error profile of the bundled model}

Tables~\ref{tab:shippederrors} and~\ref{tab:shippedconfusion} describe the
bundled INT8 model's errors on the Weinstock held-out component and on
RT-CGM, computed from the cached confirmatory-read predictions. Every
movement class is predicted 1.7 to 5.2 times as often as it occurs;
\texttt{falling\_fast}, the most over-called, pairs the highest recall
with the lowest precision (0.706 at 0.136 held-out, 0.673 at 0.214 on
RT-CGM), and \texttt{rising\_fast} has the lowest RT-CGM recall (0.483). Of
the windows the model labels with a moving class, 63\% on both cohorts
have a mean rate within $\pm1$~\mgdl/min over the next 15 minutes, while
a displayed \texttt{stable} is right in 92\% (held-out) and 94\% (RT-CGM)
of windows. Most errors fall in an adjacent class (64\% and 81\%), but
14--15\% of truly falling or rising held-out windows are labelled in the
opposite direction (9\% on RT-CGM). Directional recall is lowest when the
last reading is below 70~\mgdl\ (0.33 held-out, 0.36 RT-CGM; 0.50--0.56
in the other bands), although that band holds only 1 held-out and 96
RT-CGM \texttt{falling\_fast} windows, of which the model recalls 0 and
2; without that class the $\geq$250~\mgdl\ band is lowest. Source:
\path{panel_revision_r1.json}.
